HOW IT WORKS

Leduc hold'em is a small two-player poker game with six cards and one shared public card. The CFR+ bot plays a table of probabilities computed offline by counterfactual regret minimization (CFR), and its faster variant CFR+. During play the server only looks up those probabilities, so no search runs while a hand is in progress.

The game: Leduc hold'em

The deck has six cards: two each of J, Q and K 1. Each seat puts in an ante of 1 chip 2 and is dealt one private card face down 3. Round 1 betting follows with bets of 2 chips. A public card is then turned up for both seats, and round 2 betting follows with bets of 4 chips 4. Seat 0 acts first in both rounds 5.

In each round, a check passes when nothing is owed, and a bet opens the betting. Once a bet is owed, a call matches it and closes the round, a raise adds one bet size on top of it, and a fold gives up the pot and ends the hand 67. This is fixed-limit betting, where every bet is one fixed size. At most two bets or raises are allowed in a round 8.

A seat's put is its ante plus every bet and call it has made. A call matches the outstanding amount, and a bet or raise sets the raiser's total to the highest total so far plus one bet size 910.

At the showdown, a private card that matches the public card makes a pair, and a pair beats any hand without one. Otherwise the higher rank wins, and equal ranks split the pot 11. Each rank has two cards, so a pair means the other card of the public card's rank.

The payoff is the net chips for seat 0, written u_0. The game is zero-sum: seat 1's payoff is the negative of seat 0's, so one seat's gain is exactly the other's loss. Seat 0 is you in play mode and the CFR+ AI in watch mode.

u_0=\begin{cases}\mathrm{put}_1 & \text{seat 1 folded, or seat 0 wins the showdown}\\ -\mathrm{put}_0 & \text{seat 0 folded, or seat 1 wins the showdown}\\ 0 & \text{split pot}\end{cases}
12

Here put_0 and put_1 are the two seats' puts at the end of the hand. A fold gives the other seat the pot, so the winner takes the loser's put. Seat 0 wins a showdown, and so takes seat 1's put 12.

A seat sees its own card, the public card once it is turned up, and the betting so far, and nothing else. Leduc is therefore an imperfect-information game, in which each seat's view of the hand is partial 13.

Example. You hold a J, the bot holds a K, and the public card is the other J. Both seats check in round 1. In round 2 you bet 4 and the bot calls. Each seat's put is 5 chips, so the pot is 10. Your J pairs the public card and wins, so your net is the bot's put, 5 chips 12.

The game tree and information sets

The solver works on an explicit game tree. Its root is a chance node that deals two distinct private cards. There are 30 equally likely deals, since the cards are dealt in order from six 14. Each round 1 ending that does not fold leads to a public-card chance node, where each of the four cards not held is equally likely 15. A decision node is a point where a seat chooses an action, and a terminal node ends the hand and carries its payoff 16.

The tree has 9,451 nodes: 3,780 decision nodes, 151 chance nodes and 5,520 terminal nodes. The 151 chance nodes are the deal, plus one public-card node for each of the 30 deals after each of the five round 1 endings that do not fold: kk, bc, brc, kbc and kbrc.

An information set is the set of histories that one seat cannot tell apart. Its key is the seat's card, the public card once shown (or -), and the betting. For example, K|-|kb is a king facing a bet after a round 1 check, with the public card still hidden 13. Every history in an information set shares one strategy. The tree checks that every node in a set offers the same actions and sits at the same depth 1718. Leduc has 288 information sets 19.

Regret and regret matching

A regret for an action is how much better that action would have done, summed over the iterations, than the mixed strategy the seat played at an information set. The solver keeps one regret per action at each information set 20.

The update uses counterfactual values. Each history is weighted by the probability that the other seat and chance lead to it, not by the seat's own probability. So the regret measures the change in the seat's value from that information set alone 21. An iteration's regret delta for an action is:

d_t(I,a)=\sum_{h\in I}\pi_{-i}(h)\bigl(v_t(h a)-v_t(h)\bigr),\qquad v_t(h)=\sum_{b\in A(I)}\sigma_t(I,b)\,v_t(h b)
21

Here I is an information set of the seat, h ranges over its histories, a is an action, \sigma_t(I,b) is the probability of action b at I on iteration t, A(I) is the set of legal actions there, and \pi_{-i}(h) is the probability that the other seat and chance lead to h. The code's pi_other is this weight, and vals and node_value are v_t(ha) and v_t(h) 21.

The cumulative regret R_t(I,a) adds this delta. Vanilla CFR keeps negative regrets, and CFR+ floors them at zero (see the next topic) 22:

R_t(I,a)=R_{t-1}(I,a)+d_t(I,a)\quad\text{(vanilla CFR)},\qquad R_t(I,a)=\max\bigl(R_{t-1}(I,a)+d_t(I,a),\,0\bigr)\quad\text{(CFR+)}
22

Regret matching turns the positive regrets into the strategy for the next iteration. An action is played in proportion to its positive regret, and every action is equally likely when none has positive regret. Here R^{+}=\max(R,0) and |A(I)| is the number of legal actions 23.

\sigma_t(I,a)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{R_t^{+}(I,a)}{\sum_{b\in A(I)}R_t^{+}(I,b)} & \text{if the denominator is positive}\\[6pt] \dfrac{1}{|A(I)|} & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}
23

The uniform fallback lets a new information set start with no preference. Pseudocode for the rule follows.

23 
function REGRET-MATCHING(regret):
    positive ← each regret with negatives set to 0
    if the positives sum to more than 0:
        return each positive divided by that sum
    return the uniform distribution over the actions
function REGRET-MATCHING(regret):
    for each action a:
        pos(a) ← max(regret(a), 0)          # R⁺: only positive regret counts
    total ← the sum of pos(a) over all actions
    if total > 0:
        return pos(a) / total for each action a
    return 1 / (number of actions) for each action a

CFR+ and the average strategy

CFR+ is a variant of CFR that makes two changes. First, the cumulative regrets are floored at zero, so an action that was bad for a long time can recover quickly 22. Second, iteration t is weighted by t in the average strategy, which is the linear averaging rule 24. Later strategies are closer to equilibrium, so they count more. Vanilla CFR gives every iteration weight 1 and keeps negative regrets 24.

The strategy that the bot plays is the average, not the current strategy. Each iteration adds the strategy it played to a running sum, and the average is the normalized sum 25:

S_T(I,a)=\sum_{t=1}^{T} w_t\sum_{h\in I}\pi_i(h)\,\sigma_t(I,a),\qquad \bar\sigma_T(I,a)=\frac{S_T(I,a)}{\sum_{b\in A(I)}S_T(I,b)}
2526

Here \pi_i(h) is the seat's own probability of reaching h, which is the weight that makes the average follow the seat's own play. The weight w_t is t for CFR+ and 1 for vanilla CFR, and \bar\sigma_T is uniform when the denominator is zero 25.

Each iteration makes two walks, one for each seat. A walk updates the regrets and the average of that seat's information sets only. The walk for seat 1 runs after seat 0's regrets are updated, so it responds to seat 0's newest strategy, not to the strategy from the start of the iteration 27.

2728 
function CFR-PLUS(tree, iterations):
    repeat iterations times:
        for each seat in 0 and 1:
            σ ← REGRET-MATCHING(regret) for every information set
            WALK(root, seat)                   # regret and average of this seat
            floor the regrets of this seat's information sets at 0
    return the average strategy σ̄              # weighted by t, normalized
function CFR-PLUS(tree, iterations):
    for t in 1 to iterations:
        w ← t                                  # CFR+ weights iteration t by t (vanilla: w ← 1)
        for seat in 0, 1:
            σ ← REGRET-MATCHING(R(I)) for every information set I
            D ← 0 for every (I, a)             # regret deltas of this walk
            WALK(root, seat, 1, 1, σ, D, w)
            for each information set I of seat:
                for each action a of I:
                    R(I, a) ← max(R(I, a) + D(I, a), 0)   # CFR+ floor (vanilla: no max)
        if t is a checkpoint:
            record t and EXPLOITABILITY(σ̄)     # see the best-response section
    return σ̄

function WALK(n, seat, π_seat, π_other, σ, D, w):
    if n is terminal:
        return u_0(n) if seat = 0, otherwise −u_0(n)
    if n is a chance node:
        return the sum over children c of p(c) · WALK(c, seat, π_seat, π_other · p(c), σ, D, w)
    I ← the information set of n;  s ← σ(I)
    if the player at n is not seat:
        return the sum over actions a of s(a) · WALK(child, seat, π_seat, π_other · s(a), σ, D, w)
    for each action a_j with child c_j:
        v_j ← WALK(c_j, seat, π_seat · s(a_j), π_other, σ, D, w)
    v ← the sum over j of s(a_j) · v_j            # value of n under σ
    for each action a_j:
        D(I, a_j) ← D(I, a_j) + π_other · (v_j − v)   # counterfactual regret
        S(I, a_j) ← S(I, a_j) + w · π_seat · s(a_j)   # average strategy numerator
    return v

Reach is the probability that a path is followed. The walk carries two reaches: pi_seat for the seat's own moves and pi_other for the opponent's moves and chance. Regrets use only pi_other, and the average uses only pi_seat. This split is what makes the regret counterfactual.

A seat's own move is not weighted in its regret, because the regret asks what each action would have earned. The seat's own choice enters only the average, which records how often the seat actually plays each action.

The floor in CFR+ is applied after each walk, to the cumulative regrets. The average weight w is the iteration number, so the strategies from late iterations dominate the average.

Exploitability and best response

A best response is the strategy that gives one seat the highest payoff against a fixed strategy of the other seat. The exploitability of a profile (one strategy per seat) is how much the two best responses gain against it on average. It is zero exactly at a Nash equilibrium, the profile where neither seat gains by deviating 29.

The game value v is seat 0's expected payoff under the profile. Seat 1's expected payoff is -v. So seat 0's gain from its best response is b_0 - v, and seat 1's is b_1 + v, where b_1 is seat 1's best response value in its own payoff. The two gains add to b_0 + b_1, and the average is the exploitability 29.

e(\bar\sigma)=\tfrac12\bigl(b_0+b_1\bigr)
29

A best response cannot be found by picking the best child at each node. A seat must choose one action per information set, the same at every history in it. The code uses two passes over the tree. The forward pass computes the reach of each node from the opponent and chance, and the backward pass, deepest level first, chooses each information set's action by its counterfactual value 30:

\mathrm{CFV}(I,a)=\sum_{h\in I}\rho(h)\,b(h a),\qquad a^{\star}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{a}\,\mathrm{CFV}(I,a)
31

Here \rho(h) is the reach of h from the opponent and chance, and b(h) is the best response value from h. The reach update is \rho(c)=\rho(n)\,p(c) at a chance node, \rho(c)=\rho(n)\,\sigma(I(n),a) at an opponent node, and \rho(c)=\rho(n) at the seat's own node 32. The value of a terminal node is \pm u_0, the sign being + for seat 0 33.

34 
function BEST-RESPONSE(tree, σ, seat):
    reach ← chance and the opponent's probability of reaching each node
    for each level from the deepest to the root:
        terminal: value ← the payoff for seat
        chance or opponent: value ← the average over children
        seat's information set I: for each action a, CFV(I, a) ← the sum of reach(h) · value(h·a)
            every node of I takes the value of the action with the largest CFV
    return value(root)
function BEST-RESPONSE(tree, σ, seat):
    reach(root) ← 1
    for each node n from the root down:                 # parents before children
        if n is a chance node:
            for each child c: reach(c) ← reach(c) + reach(n) · p(c)
        else if the player at n is not seat:
            for each action a with child c: reach(c) ← reach(c) + reach(n) · σ(I(n), a)
        else:                                          # the seat's own move
            for each child c: reach(c) ← reach(c) + reach(n)
    sign ← +1 if seat = 0, otherwise −1
    for each level from the deepest to the root:
        own ← empty map from information set to nodes
        for each node n in the level:
            if n is terminal:
                value(n) ← sign · u_0(n)
            else if n is a chance node:
                value(n) ← the sum over children c of p(c) · value(c)
            else if the player at n is not seat:
                value(n) ← the sum over actions a of σ(I(n), a) · value(child)
            else:
                add n to own[I(n)]
        for each information set I in own:
            CFV(I, a) ← 0 for each action a
            for each node n in own[I]:
                for each action a with child c: CFV(I, a) ← CFV(I, a) + reach(n) · value(c)
            best ← the first action with the largest CFV(I, a)
            for each node n in own[I]:
                value(n) ← value(child of n for action best)
    return value(root)
29 
function EXPLOITABILITY(tree, σ):
    b_0 ← BEST-RESPONSE(tree, σ, seat 0)
    b_1 ← BEST-RESPONSE(tree, σ, seat 1)       # seat 1's own payoff
    return (b_0 + b_1) / 2
function EXPLOITABILITY(tree, σ):
    b_0 ← BEST-RESPONSE(tree, σ, 0)
    b_1 ← BEST-RESPONSE(tree, σ, 1)            # already in seat 1's own payoff
    return (b_0 + b_1) / 2, b_0, b_1

Two details matter in the backward pass. The reach excludes the seat's own actions, because the seat is choosing them, so the choice at each information set maximizes the value weighted only by the opponent's reach. A tie goes to the first action in the list, so the result is deterministic 31.

The committed strategy has exploitability 0.00010694 chips per hand. Since the big blind is 2 chips, that is about 0.053 milli-big-blinds per hand. It is measured on the rounded table, the one the bot plays 3519.

Why mixing is the point. If the bot never bluffed, you could fold every bet and lose nothing. If it always bluffed, you could call every bet and win with every Q and K. At equilibrium its bluff frequency leaves you indifferent between calling and folding, so neither choice can be exploited. That is the sense in which the mix is optimal.

The CFR+ bot

The CFR+ bot plays the committed average strategy. The table maps each information set key to probabilities over its legal actions 36. It is rounded to four decimals and renormalized, so each row sums to exactly one 37. Its move is a lookup and a draw from that row 38.

In play mode the bot is seat 1. It sees only its own card and the betting, since its information set key has no view of your card 13. The server holds the bot's card and each decision's mix back until the hand ends. Each response carries only the bot's actions, its round, and its spot, which is its information set with the card replaced by ? 3940.

The spot lets the explainer look up the bot's bet rate for every card in the same betting spot. Its bet rate for a card is the sum of the bet and raise probabilities in that row 41. The strategy table is public at /api/poker/strategy, so this adds no information beyond the committed table. At the showdown, the response adds the bot's card and every decision with its mix 42. The page shows the bot's card face down until that point 43.

Chance (fixed odds)

Chance is the second opponent. It is not a solver. It draws each action from fixed odds and never reads its card, searches, or looks ahead 44. The odds are fold 15%, call or check 55%, and bet or raise 30%, the same at every decision in both rounds and for both seats 45.

  • Fold 15%, call or check 55%, bet or raise 30%, renormalized over the legal actions.

At each decision, chance keeps only the legal actions, weights each by the odds for its kind, renormalizes over the legal set, and samples one with a seeded random generator 46. The weight of a legal action a is:

w(a)=\frac{p_{\kappa(a)}}{\sum_{b\in L}p_{\kappa(b)}},\qquad p_{\text{fold}}=0.15,\quad p_{\text{call or check}}=0.55,\quad p_{\text{bet or raise}}=0.30
4546

Here L is the set of legal actions and \kappa(a) is the kind of a. Check and call are one kind, and bet and raise are one kind 47. Example. Facing a bet, the legal set is fold, call and raise, so the weights are 0.15, 0.55 and 0.30, which already sum to one. At the raise cap, the raise is removed, and fold and call become 0.15 / 0.70 and 0.55 / 0.70 46.

A draw is made by inverse sampling: draw a uniform number r from [0,1), then take the first action whose cumulative probability exceeds it. The dict order is fixed, so a seeded generator repeats exactly 48.

4948 
function CHANCE-ACT(state, rng):
    legal ← the legal actions at state
    for each action a in legal:
        raw(a) ← the odds for the kind of a
    weights ← raw(a) divided by the sum of raw over legal
    return SAMPLE(weights, rng)
function CHANCE-ACT(state, rng):
    legal ← the legal actions at state
    for each action a in legal:
        raw(a) ← ODDS[KIND(a)]         # fold 0.15, check or call 0.55, bet or raise 0.30
    total ← the sum of raw(a) over legal
    for each action a in legal:
        w(a) ← raw(a) / total          # renormalized over the legal set
    return SAMPLE(w, rng)

function SAMPLE(row, rng):
    r ← a uniform random number in [0, 1)
    acc ← 0
    for each action a with probability p in row, in dict order:
        acc ← acc + p
        last ← a
        if r < acc:
            return a
    return last                        # float round-off can leave acc just below 1

The same sampler is used by the CFR+ bot, so both opponents are drawn the same way. The guard after the loop matters because the probabilities add up to one only up to floating point error. Without it, a draw of r just below one could fall through the loop.

In watch mode the browser draws the CFR+ AI's moves with the same rule, from the row the server sends for its turn 50. Each watch tick makes one request, and the page schedules the next tick 51.

Baselines and the benchmark

Three baselines give yardsticks. Random picks uniformly from the legal actions 52. Always-call calls any bet and checks when nothing is owed, so it never folds or bets 53. Hand-strength is a fixed rule that reads only the seat's own card and the public card once it is shown 54:

Own cardRule
Pair with the public cardBet when unbet; facing a bet, raise if legal, otherwise call
King, no pairBet; call a bet
Queen, no pairCheck; call a bet
Jack, no pairCheck; fold to a bet

The hand-strength rules are hand-picked, a plain reading of the card ranks, and they are not tuned. The benchmark plays each deal twice, once in each seat, so the luck of the cards cancels. It uses seeded deals, 100,000 per matchup (200,000 hands each), and reports winnings in milli-big-blinds per hand 5556.

For deal k, let x_k be the agent's net chips when it sits in seat 0, and y_k its net chips when it sits in seat 1. The per-hand figure for that deal is the average of the two seats:

d_k=\tfrac12\,(x_k+y_k),\qquad \bar d=\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_k,\qquad s^2=\frac{n}{n-1}\Bigl(\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_k^{2}-\bar d^{\,2}\Bigr),\qquad \mathrm{se}=\sqrt{s^2/n}
56

Here n is the number of deals, \bar d is the mean chips per hand, and s^2 is the sample variance of the per-deal figures. The variance is clamped at zero before the scaling, so rounding cannot make it negative. The standard error \mathrm{se} measures the uncertainty of the mean 56.

\text{mbb/hand}=\frac{1000}{2}\,\bar d,\qquad \text{95\% CI}=\text{mbb/hand}\pm 1.96\cdot\frac{1000}{2}\,\mathrm{se}
5758

A milli-big-blind is a thousandth of a big blind. The big blind is the round 1 bet, 2 chips, so the factor is 1000 / 2 59. The interval is a 95% confidence interval using the normal value 1.96 57. Committed results over 200,000 hands each are below. The self-play row should be close to zero, since neither side can win in expectation at equilibrium 60.

Training and results

The strategy file is produced with python -m poker train --save --iterations 5000. It runs CFR and CFR+ on Leduc for 5,000 iterations each, then writes the strategy, the curves and the log. Without --iterations the command runs 10,000 6162.

Exploitability is computed exactly by best response, not sampled. It is measured at checkpoints spaced roughly logarithmically: with 40 points, the checkpoint at index i is the iteration nearest T^{i/39}, plus iterations 1 and T 63. The committed curves have 37 distinct iterations each 64.

m_i=\operatorname{round}\!\left(T^{\,i/39}\right),\qquad i=0,\dots,39
63

The committed CFR+ run took 230.6 seconds. Its game value for seat 0 is −0.085606 chips. The best responses are −0.08552004 and 0.08573392, so the exploitability is (-0.08552004+0.08573392)/2 = 0.00010694 chips per hand 19.

Vanilla CFR, with the same checkpoints, ends at exploitability 0.00358651 chips after 5,000 iterations. CFR+ ends at about 0.0000148, which shows the effect of the floor and linear averaging 6465.

Kuhn poker is a three-card game with a known game value of −1/18 for the first seat 66. Trained on Kuhn for 10,000 iterations, CFR+ lands 3.6e-9 from that value, and vanilla CFR lands 8.0e-6 from it. This checks the solver on a game whose answer is known exactly 67.

The number of iterations and the algorithm were chosen rather than swept, since the repository has no sweep over iterations or game settings. The Leduc rules are the ones coded in the game module. The probabilities are rounded to four decimals so the file stays small 37.

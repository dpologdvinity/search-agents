HOW IT THINKS
SINGLE-CELL RULES
A 1 with one covered neighbour makes that neighbour a mine. A number its known mines already satisfy makes every other covered neighbour safe. Each new fact can trigger more.
COMPONENTS
Numbers that share a covered cell form one component. Components are independent once the total is fixed, so the agent counts each on its own and enumerates every layout that fits.
THE MINE COUNT
The mines left over go among the covered cells that touch no number, in C(n, k) ways. A layout counts only if its total matches the board, which can prove cells the numbers alone cannot.
EXACT ODDS
A cell's odds are the layouts where it is a mine, over all layouts. When nothing is certain, the agent guesses the lowest, breaking ties toward cells whose zero would open the most board.

HOW IT WORKS

The agent treats each revealed number as a constraint on the covered squares around it. It makes every move that the counting proves safe. When nothing is proven, it guesses the covered square with the lowest exact chance of being a mine, counted over every layout of mines that fits the numbers and the mine count.

Single-square rules (level 1)

New to this? This is the reasoning you do one clue at a time. If a 1 touches exactly one covered square, that square must hold the mine. Each new discovery can unlock another clue, so the rules repeat until nothing new turns up.

rule_pass marks every covered neighbour of a number as a mine when the number needs as many mines as it has covered neighbours. It marks them safe when the mines already known satisfy the number. It repeats to a fixpoint. If nothing is proven, it guesses a covered square at random.

Constraint groups and exact counts (level 2)

New to this? Each number is a sentence such as "exactly two of these three squares are mines". Sentences that share a square have to be solved together. The agent counts every way the sentences can all be true, much as you might count the ways to fill a crossword grid.

Constraints that share a covered square form a group. Each group is counted by a backtracking search that remembers repeated states. A square is proven safe when no consistent layout puts a mine on it, and proven a mine when every layout does. The total mine count combines the groups. If nothing is proven, the guess is random.

Exact mine probability (level 3, default)

New to this? Treat every layout of mines that fits the clues as equally likely. A square's chance of being a mine is the share of those layouts that put a mine on it. It is like checking every seating plan that obeys a rule, then asking how often a particular seat is taken.

Covered squares that touch no number (the interior) take the leftover mines in C(n, k) ways. All weights are integers until the final division, so comparisons are exact. When nothing is proven, the agent guesses the covered square with the lowest probability. The count has a work budget: a board too wide to count within it keeps its proofs, shows estimated odds instead, and the page says so.

  • Ties go to the square with the most covered neighbours, since revealing it would open the most, then to the lowest cell number.

Random (baseline)

New to this? Pick any covered square at random, with no reasoning at all. It is the floor that the other agents are measured against.

  • On beginner boards it wins 0.0% (0 of 1,000 games) and needs 3.36 guesses per game.

How these were chosen: nothing is tuned. The probabilities are exact counts, and the proof rules follow from the rules of the game. The tie-breaks were fixed by hand, not tuned. The three presets are the classic beginner (9×9, 10 mines), intermediate (16×16, 40) and expert (30 columns by 16 rows, 99) boards. The benchmark plays 1,000, 500 and 200 seeded games per size, on seeds 10,000 onward, with the same boards for every agent.

Results (95% Wilson intervals): on beginner boards the probability agent wins 97.5% against 96.1% for the constraint agent and 79.8% for the rules agent. On intermediate boards the figures are 87.8%, 85.4% and 49.2%. On expert boards they are 48.5%, 39.0% and 1.5%. The probability agent guesses more on expert boards (2.88 per game against 2.29), since it keeps playing longer.

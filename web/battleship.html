    HOW IT CHOOSES
    COUNTING LAYOUTS
    For every cell it asks: in how many ways can the ships still afloat be placed so that they fit its misses, its hits, and the ships it has sunk? The share of those layouts that use a cell is that cell's odds.
    A HIT IS A CLUE
    A hit on a ship that is not yet sunk means a ship runs through that cell, so its neighbours rise. Two aligned hits show the ship's direction.
    COUNTING OR SAMPLING
    Early on there are billions of layouts, so it samples them at random and weights each sample to keep the odds fair. Late in a game it counts every layout exactly.
    CHANCE: FIXED ODDS
    Chance never reads its hits or misses. Its table is fixed in advance, so the shooter is a dice roller and has no reasoning. Centre cells lie under more ship placements, which is why the table favours them.

    HOW IT WORKS

    The opponent fires at your fleet, and you fire at its fleet. A Bayesian opponent counts the fleet layouts that still fit what it has seen, then fires at the square that most of them cover. A chance opponent fires from a fixed table, whatever it has seen. In watch mode the AI and the opponent race each other's fleets, and the first fleet sunk loses.

    Bayesian probability targeting

    New to this? Bayesian reasoning means updating a guess as evidence comes in. Picture drawing marbles from a bag one at a time: each one changes which colour mix still seems likely. Each miss and hit here rules out some fleet layouts, and the agent bets on the square most surviving layouts agree on.

    The agent is the only one on this page. It keeps every legal placement of the ships still afloat that fits the evidence, and sets P(ship) = (kept layouts covering the cell) / (all kept layouts). It fires at the highest value; ties are broken at random.

    • A layout is kept only if no ship covers a miss or a sunk ship, every open hit is covered, and no two ships overlap.
    • Every legal fleet is equally likely to start with, so each kept layout counts the same. The page's random fleets are drawn the same way.
    • Neighbours of an open hit rise on their own, because the ship through that hit must cover one of them.

    Exact counting

    New to this? Counting every possibility looks hopeless, but a systematic search can skip most of them. It is like seating dinner guests one at a time and abandoning any arrangement where the remaining chairs cannot seat the people still waiting.

    A depth-first search places the remaining ships one by one and drops a branch when the open hits still uncovered outnumber the cells the remaining ships can cover. Exact counting is tried only when the placements, ignoring overlaps, number 30,000 or fewer, and is abandoned past 60,000 candidate checks. While both 3-cell ships are afloat, the search treats them as ordered, so each layout counts twice; the odds are unaffected.

    Importance sampling

    New to this? When a full list is too big, estimate from a random sample and weight each sample by how likely it was to be drawn. A pollster who surveys only one train station can correct for that by weighting answers by the station's traffic.

    Early on there are too many layouts to count, so the agent draws 1,000 at random. Each ship goes uniformly on a placement that avoids used cells, and the sample's weight is multiplied by the number of options at each step. Samples that leave an open hit uncovered get weight 0. P(cell) is the weighted share of samples covering it.

    Chance (fixed odds)

    New to this? Like a die with fixed faces: the odds are printed on the die before the game starts, and rolling it tells you nothing about the board. Chance is the Battleship version of the 2048 tile spawner, which places a 2 nine times in ten and a 4 one time in ten whatever is on the board.

    Each cell has a fixed weight, profile[row] × profile[col], with the profile (1, 2, 3, 4, 4, 4, 4, 3, 2, 1) rising to the middle. It fires at an unknown cell drawn in proportion to that weight, renormalised over the cells it has not fired at. No search, no lookahead, and no use of hits or misses. Ships are placed uniformly, so a centre cell does lie under more placements, which is why the middle is favoured.

    Hunt and target (baseline)

    New to this? This is how most people play by hand: search a pattern until something is hit, then chase the wounded ship.

    Used only by the benchmark, not the page. It fires on cells where row + column is even until it scores a hit. Every ship of length 2 or more covers an even cell, so one colour is always hit. After a hit it fires at unknown neighbours, and once two open hits share a row or column, only along that line.

    Random (baseline)

    New to this? Like drawing from a shuffled deck: every card left is equally likely, and the cards already drawn do not change the odds of the next one. It is the floor any real strategy should beat.

    Fires at a uniformly random unknown cell. It ignores every hit.

    How these were chosen: CHANCE_PROFILE is a hand-picked shape: symmetric, rising from the edge to the middle, with four equal middle rows. Nothing is learned or tuned. The probabilities follow from the rules as a count under a uniform prior. SAMPLES and EXACT_BUDGET are hand-picked constants; the repository has no sweep that justifies them. The 30,000 gate is half the budget, so an exact attempt can finish before it runs out. The checkerboard follows from the ship lengths, not from a search.

    Results

    100 random fleets, seed 1, the same fleets for every agent. Mean shots to sink the fleet: random 95.4, hunt and target 51.9, Bayesian 45.9 (median 45.5, worst 64). The Bayesian agent needed fewer shots than hunt and target on 67 of 100 fleets, and 6.0 fewer on average (95% interval 3.7 to 8.2), at a much higher cost per shot.

    Race, 100 games, seed 1, python -m battleship match --games 100 --seed 1: Bayesian AI 100 wins, chance 0. Hunt and target vs chance: the same 100 to 0. Races cannot be drawn, since the first fleet sunk decides. Full output in results/battleship_match.md.

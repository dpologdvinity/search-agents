LOST ROBOT

A robot on a known floor plan does not know where it is. Two filters estimate its position from noisy range sensors and drifting wheels.

HOW TO USE

The robot starts at a random place on the floor, and both filters start knowing nothing about where it is. Its range sensors measure the distance to the nearest wall in eight directions by default, and its wheels drift.

Press AUTOPILOT to let the robot drive itself, or steer with the arrow keys or WASD. KIDNAP carries the robot to a new place without telling the filters.

HOW IT WORKS

A filter combines the commands a robot sends its wheels, which it knows, with the readings of its range sensor, which are noisy, into a running estimate of where the robot is. This page runs two filters on the same robot and the same beams. Both start from total ignorance. On 16 seeded trials, the 2000-particle filter holds within 0.5 m for ten steps in 14 of them; the exact grid filter does so in all 16, but takes about 2.5 times as long per step in this implementation. Two devices do most of the work: the particles are resampled when their weights bunch up, and a fraction is re-scattered over the floor when the beams stop matching the belief.

The floor, the robot and its sensors

The floor is a grid of 24 by 16 cells, and each cell is 0.5 m on a side, so the floor measures 12 m by 8 m.1 Walls fill whole cells, and the robot itself is a point. Two plans are offered. Twin Halls has six rooms that look the same from inside, so the belief can split between them until a feature shows up. The Vault is an irregular plan with no symmetry.2

Each plan has a few pillar slots, single cells that may or may not hold a pillar. The seed draws one uniform number per slot, and a slot holds a pillar when the draw is below 0.5. The same plan and seed therefore always give the same floor.3 The robot is placed in the region, the largest connected block of free cells, so that it never starts in a pocket cut off by a pillar.4

A pose is the robot's state: its position and heading (x, y, θ). The position is in cells, and the heading θ is in radians, measured from the +x direction. Between two ticks the robot is commanded a turn r and a drive f. Both commands are noisy. The standard deviations of that noise grow with the size of the command, and the odometry noise slider multiplies both.5

5
\sigma_r = s\,(0.05 + 0.15\,|r|) \qquad \sigma_f = s\,(0.03 + 0.10\,|f|)

Here s is the odometry noise multiplier (1.0 by default), r is the commanded turn in radians, f is the commanded drive in cells, and \sigma_r and \sigma_f are the standard deviations of the turn and drive noise. For a turn of 0.25 rad and a drive of 0.3 cells, the noise is 0.0875 rad and 0.06 cells.

The motion itself is simple. The robot drives along its heading, then turns. If the end of the drive lands in a wall or outside the floor, the robot bumps: it keeps its position but still turns. Steps are shorter than a cell, so a robot cannot pass through a one-cell wall in a single move. The heading is wrapped into the range minus pi to pi.6

7
x' = x + f\cos\theta,\quad y' = y + f\sin\theta,\quad \theta' = \operatorname{wrap}(\theta + r)

If the end point (x', y') is inside a wall or outside the floor, x' and y' are replaced by x and y. The function \operatorname{wrap}(a) = a - 2\pi\lfloor (a + \pi) / 2\pi \rfloor maps any angle into the range from minus pi up to pi.8 It is written with a floor so that the JavaScript port gives the same value.

The sensor has R beams, 8 by default, spaced evenly around the full circle and starting from the robot's heading. Beam i points at the angle below. Each beam reports the distance to the first wall it meets, up to a maximum range of 8 cells (4 m).9

9
\theta_i = \theta + \dfrac{2\pi\, i}{R}, \qquad i = 0, 1, \dots, R-1

The distance is found by ray casting. The ray is marched forward in steps of 0.25 cells until one step lands inside a wall or outside the floor, and the distance is the step count times 0.25. The distance is therefore quantised to 0.25 cells, and a ray that finds nothing reads 8. All rays advance together, which keeps the cast fast enough for thousands of particles.10

10 
function RAY-CAST(walls, x, y, angle):
    for k in 1 to 32:                              # march in steps of 0.25 cells, up to 8 cells
        (x, y) ← (x, y) + 0.25 × (cos angle, sin angle)
        if (x, y) is in a wall or outside the floor:
            return k × 0.25                        # distance = steps × step length
    return 8                                       # nothing hit: the maximum range
function RAY-CAST(walls, x, y, angle):
    dx ← 0.25 × cos(angle)                         # one step of the march
    dy ← 0.25 × sin(angle)
    d ← 8                                          # MAX-RANGE, kept if no wall is reached
    hit ← false
    k ← 0
    repeat 32 times:
        k ← k + 1
        x ← x + dx
        y ← y + dy
        if not hit and (x, y) is in a wall or outside the floor:
            d ← k × 0.25                           # the first hit fixes the distance
            hit ← true
        if hit:
            stop                                   # nothing after the first hit can change d
    return d

The simple version marches one ray until it meets a wall. The full version uses the same march, with the step vector split into its two parts. Once a ray has hit, its distance is fixed, so the loop can stop early without changing the result. The code applies this to all rays at once and stops when every ray has hit.

The ray cast also builds the grid filter's table of expected ranges, and it runs inside every particle's sensing step.

The real sensor adds noise. A reading is the true distance d plus Gaussian noise with standard deviation \sigma, clipped to the range from 0 to 8. With probability p_d, a dropout, the reading is 8 instead. The defaults are \sigma = 0.3 cells (0.15 m) and p_d = 0.05.11

11
z = \begin{cases} 8 & \text{with probability } p_d \\ \operatorname{clip}(d + \sigma n,\ 0,\ 8) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}

Here n is a standard normal draw. The filters' likelihood below ignores the clip at zero, which matters only when the robot is within about one noise standard deviation of a wall.

The autopilot drives the true robot along shortest routes. It picks a random destination in the region and finds the route with a breadth-first search, which explores cells in rings of increasing distance from the start, so the first route that reaches the goal has the fewest steps. Neighbours are tried in the order north, east, south, west, so ties break the same way every run.12 It steers toward each waypoint with a turn of at most 0.25 rad per tick, and it drives 0.3 cells per tick only once it faces the waypoint to within 0.5 rad. A waypoint counts as reached within 0.35 cells.13

14
e = \operatorname{wrap}\big(\operatorname{atan2}(t_y - y,\ t_x - x) - \theta\big), \qquad r = \operatorname{clip}(e,\ -0.25,\ 0.25), \qquad f = \begin{cases} 0.3 & \text{if } |e| < 0.5 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}

Here (t_x, t_y) is the centre of the next waypoint. The autopilot steers by the true pose, which a real robot's controller would know about its own motion. The filters are not given that pose. They receive only the commanded turn and drive, and the beams.

Bayes' rule: belief times evidence

Bayes' rule is the update that combines a prior with evidence. The belief is the filter's probability distribution over where the robot is. Before a reading, the belief is the prior. Each sensor reading is evidence, and the likelihood of a reading is how probable that reading is at each pose. The new belief, the posterior, is the prior times the likelihood, divided by a normaliser that makes the posterior add up to one.

15
p(x \mid z) = \dfrac{p(z \mid x)\; p(x)}{p(z)}

Here x is the pose and z is the set of beams. The term p(z) is the sum over all poses that makes the posterior add to one. The filters never compute it. They scale by the largest likelihood before taking exponentials and normalise at the end, which gives the same posterior.16

Each tick has two parts. The predict step moves the belief by the commanded motion, including its noise. The noise makes the belief wider. The update step multiplies the belief by the likelihood of the beams. The sensor makes it narrower. A filter with no sensor would only spread out, and a filter with no odometry could only ever be where its first scan put it.

The beams are treated as independent, so the likelihood of a whole set of beams is a product of per-beam likelihoods. The product is computed as a sum of logarithms, because eight or sixteen small numbers multiply to something that underflows quickly. The expected range e_i(x) is the ray-cast distance for beam i from pose x.

17
\ell(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{R} \log L\big(z_i \mid e_i(x)\big)

The per-beam likelihood L(z \mid e) has two cases, because a reading of 8 is a "no return" that a dropout or a long true range can both produce.

17
L(z \mid e) = \begin{cases} (1 - p_d)\,\dfrac{\exp\!\big(-\tfrac{(z - e)^2}{2\sigma^2}\big)}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} + \dfrac{\varepsilon}{8} & \text{if } z < 8 \\[2ex] p_d + (1 - p_d)\cdot\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}\!\Big(\dfrac{8 - e}{\sigma\sqrt2}\Big) + \dfrac{\varepsilon}{8} & \text{if } z = 8 \end{cases}

Here e is the expected range, \sigma = 0.3 is the sensor noise, p_d = 0.05 is the dropout probability, and \varepsilon = 0.02 is the weight of a uniform "unexpected reading" term. The term \varepsilon / 8 is that uniform density over the range from 0 to 8, so one surprising beam cannot erase a pose completely. In the second case, the erfc term is the chance that noise pushed a true range beyond 8. The code evaluates erfc with a polynomial approximation whose error is below 1.2 × 10⁻⁷, so that JavaScript computes the same numbers.18

Example. A beam with expected range 4 cells and a reading of 4.2 cells, with \sigma = 0.3, is 0.2 above the expectation. That is two thirds of a sigma, so its Gaussian factor is about 0.8 of the peak. A reading one cell away from its expectation is 3.3 sigma off, so its Gaussian factor is about 0.004 of the peak. The uniform term \varepsilon / 8 keeps the factor above zero.

Grid filter: one number per bin

The grid filter is also called a histogram filter. It cuts the state space into bins and keeps one probability per bin. A bin is a quarter-cell position (0.25 m, so two positions per cell along each axis) times one of 16 headings, 22.5° apart. The floor therefore has 48 × 32 × 16 = 24 576 bins. Bins inside walls are given zero probability.19 The belief starts uniform over every free bin, which is what "the robot could be anywhere" means.20

The predict step uses a motion kernel: the probability of landing in each bin, given the command. The noise is Gaussian in turn and drive, and the kernel's integral over each bin is approximated with Gauss–Hermite quadrature. A quadrature rule replaces an integral with a weighted sum at a few fixed points. Here each noise dimension uses three points, at −√3, 0 and √3 standard deviations, with weights 1/6, 2/3 and 1/6. Those weights give the exact mean and variance of a standard normal. Two dimensions with three points each give nine sample poses per bin.19

21
a, b \in \{-\sqrt3,\ 0,\ \sqrt3\}, \qquad w_{a}, w_{b} \in \{\tfrac16,\ \tfrac23,\ \tfrac16\}, \qquad r_{ab} = r + a\,\sigma_r, \qquad f_{ab} = f + b\,\sigma_f

Each sample pose (r_{ab}, f_{ab}) carries the weight w_{ab} = w_a\,w_b. Its destination from bin s is the bin at the moved position, with the heading rounded to the nearest of the 16 bins. If the move bumps, the destination keeps the old position and takes the new heading.22 The predicted belief is then a sum over those destinations.

23
\mathrm{bel}'(s') = \sum_{s}\ \sum_{a,b}\ w_{a}\,w_{b}\ \mathbf{1}\big[\,d_{ab}(s) = s'\,\big]\ \mathrm{bel}(s)

Here d_{ab}(s) is the destination bin of sample pose (a, b) from bin s, and \mathbf{1}[\cdot] is 1 when its condition holds and 0 otherwise. The nine weights sum to one, so predict keeps the total probability unchanged, and the code does not renormalise after it.

The update step multiplies each bin by the likelihood of the beams. The expected ranges e_i(s) come from a table, computed once per floor, that ray-casts from every bin's centre. Each bin's log-likelihood is then compared with the largest one, and the result is normalised.24

15
\mathrm{bel}(s) \leftarrow \dfrac{\mathrm{bel}(s)\,\exp\big(\ell(s) - \ell^{*}\big)}{\sum_{s'} \mathrm{bel}(s')\,\exp\big(\ell(s') - \ell^{*}\big)}, \qquad \ell^{*} = \max_{s:\ \mathrm{bel}(s) > 0} \ell(s)

Here \ell(s) is the log-likelihood of the beams in bin s, from the formula above. Subtracting \ell^{*} keeps every exponent at or below zero, so no value overflows. Bins with zero belief stay at zero.

The estimate of a grid filter is the centre of its most probable bin. The heat map sums the belief over the 16 headings for each position.25 The cost grows with the number of bins, and every bin is updated on every tick, so the grid filter is exact but limited. In the Python benchmark it takes about 14 ms per step, against about 6 ms for the 2000-particle filter.

26 
function GRID-STEP(belief, rot, fwd, z):
    belief ← PREDICT(belief, rot, fwd)        # spread the belief by the odometry noise
    for each bin s:
        belief[s] ← belief[s] × LIKELIHOOD(z, s)   # beams that fit the bin raise it
    return belief / sum(belief)
function GRID-STEP(belief, rot, fwd, z):
    σ_r ← SCALE × (0.05 + 0.15 × |rot|)            # turn noise in radians
    σ_f ← SCALE × (0.03 + 0.10 × |fwd|)            # drive noise in cells
    new ← zeros, one entry per bin                 # predict: push the belief through the kernel
    for a in (−√3, 0, √3):
        for b in (−√3, 0, √3):
            w ← weight(a) × weight(b)              # weights 1/6, 2/3, 1/6
            r ← rot + a × σ_r                      # sampled turn
            f ← fwd + b × σ_f                      # sampled drive
            for each bin s = (x, y, θ) with belief[s] > 0:
                x' ← x + f × cos θ
                y' ← y + f × sin θ
                θ' ← wrap(θ + r)
                if (x', y') is a wall or outside the floor:
                    dest ← bin at (x, y) with heading θ'     # bump: keep the position
                else:
                    dest ← bin at (x', y') with heading θ'
                new[dest] ← new[dest] + w × belief[s]
    belief ← new
    for each bin s:                                # update: score every bin by its beams
        ℓ[s] ← 0
        for each beam i:
            ℓ[s] ← ℓ[s] + log L(z_i | e_i[s])      # e_i[s] from the ray-cast table
    ℓ* ← max of ℓ[s] over bins with belief[s] > 0
    for each bin s:
        belief[s] ← belief[s] × exp(ℓ[s] − ℓ*)    # zero stays zero
    return belief / sum(belief)

The simple version shows the idea: predict spreads the belief, and update reweights it. The full version adds the details that the code computes. The heading of a destination is the nearest of the 16 bins, so it is found by rounding the angle divided by 22.5°. The position of a destination is the bin that contains it, with the coordinates clipped to the floor. Every bin's log-likelihood is a sum over the beams, and the largest one is subtracted before exponentiating.

Particle filter: a sample of the belief

A particle filter keeps instead a set of guesses, the particles. Each particle is one pose (x, y, θ), and the set as a whole is the belief: a region with many heavy particles is a likely place for the robot. The method is called Monte Carlo localisation (MCL), after the random sampling it uses. The default is 2000 particles. Its cost per step grows with the particle count, not with the size of the floor, which is why it scales to larger maps.27

At the start, each particle is a uniform draw over the free cells and headings. Four uniform random numbers per particle choose a cell, an offset inside it, and a heading.28 Each tick then runs four stages: motion, sensing, resampling and recovery. The first two draw on the same odometry model that the true robot obeys, so the cloud spreads out the way the real uncertainty does.

In the motion stage each particle gets its own noise draws. The two standard normal draws n_{i,1} and n_{i,2} come from the random stream, and the particle moves with the motion equations above.

29
r_i = r + \sigma_r\, n_{i,1}, \qquad f_i = f + \sigma_f\, n_{i,2}

In the sensing stage each particle casts its own beams, so its expected ranges e_i depend on where that particle is. Its weight is multiplied by the likelihood of the real beams, and the weights are then normalised.30

31
w_i \leftarrow \dfrac{w_i\,\exp(\ell_i - \ell^{*})}{\sum_j w_j\,\exp(\ell_j - \ell^{*})}, \qquad \ell^{*} = \max_j \ell_j

Here \ell_i is the log-likelihood of the beams for particle i, from the formula in the Bayes section, and w_i is its weight before the step.

A small number of heavy particles makes the cloud a poor sample of the belief. The effective sample size (ESS) measures how concentrated the weights are. It equals N when all weights are equal and falls toward 1 when one particle holds nearly all the weight.32

33
\mathrm{ESS} = \dfrac{1}{\sum_i w_i^2}, \qquad \text{resample when } \mathrm{ESS} < N/2

Here N is the particle count and the weights w_i sum to one. A threshold of N/2 is the common compromise between keeping diversity and copying good particles.

Resampling: keep the good particles

Resampling redraws the cloud in proportion to the weights, so heavy particles are copied and light ones die. After resampling, every weight is reset to 1/N. The code uses systematic resampling, a low-variance method. It draws one random offset, then places N evenly spaced pointers through the cumulative weights. Each particle is copied about N times its weight, and the copies are spread as evenly as the weights allow, which adds less noise than N independent draws.34

34
u_0 = \dfrac{U}{N}, \qquad p_i = u_0 + \dfrac{i}{N}, \qquad j_i = \min\big(N - 1,\ \min\{\,j : C_j \ge p_i\,\}\big), \qquad C_j = \sum_{k \le j} w_k, \quad C_{N-1} := 1

Here U is one uniform draw on [0, 1), p_i is the i-th pointer, and j_i is the index of the particle that the i-th copy comes from. The cap at N − 1 and the forced value C_{N-1} = 1 guard against rounding error in the cumulative sum.

34 
function SYSTEMATIC-RESAMPLE(particles, weights):
    u0 ← UNIFORM() / N                          # one random offset in [0, 1/N)
    for i in 0 to N − 1:
        pointer ← u0 + i / N                    # N evenly spaced pointers
        new[i] ← the particle whose cumulative weight covers pointer
    return new, with every weight 1/N
function SYSTEMATIC-RESAMPLE(particles, weights):
    u0 ← UNIFORM() / N                          # one random offset in [0, 1/N)
    C ← running sum of weights
    C[N − 1] ← 1                                # guard against rounding
    for i in 0 to N − 1:
        pointer ← u0 + i / N
        j ← smallest index with C[j] ≥ pointer  # binary search over C
        j ← min(j, N − 1)
        new[i] ← particles[j]
    particles ← new
    weights ← 1 / N for every particle
    return particles, weights

The full version is the same as the simple one, with the cumulative sum and the binary search written out. The search finds the first particle whose cumulative weight reaches the pointer, so a particle with weight 0.3 covers 30% of the pointers.

Kidnapped robot: augmented MCL

If the robot is carried to a new place, every particle is wrong, and resampling cannot help, because it only copies particles that already exist. Augmented Monte Carlo localisation adds a detector and an injection. The detector watches the average likelihood of the beams. The injection replaces some particles with random poses over the whole floor, which gives the filter a chance to find the robot again.35

The average likelihood of a tick is the log of the mean of the particles' likelihoods. The code keeps two running averages of it in log form, a fast one that moves 10% toward each new value and a slow one that moves 1%. Both start at the first value.36

37
\bar L = \log\Big(\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_i \exp(\ell_i)\Big), \qquad \lambda_{\text{slow}} \leftarrow \lambda_{\text{slow}} + 0.01\,(\bar L - \lambda_{\text{slow}}), \qquad \lambda_{\text{fast}} \leftarrow \lambda_{\text{fast}} + 0.1\,(\bar L - \lambda_{\text{fast}})

Here \bar L is the log of the average likelihood this tick, and \lambda_{\text{fast}} and \lambda_{\text{slow}} are the two running averages. The code computes \bar L as the largest \ell_i plus the log of the mean of the scaled likelihoods, which is the same number without overflow.

When the robot is kidnapped, the fast average drops below the slow one. The injection fraction p is zero unless the fast average has fallen 2.5 nats (natural-log units) below the slow one. Past that trigger, p is the fraction by which the fast likelihood average has fallen below the slow one. The count of injected particles is k = \mathrm{round}(pN), rounded half to even as Python does.

38
p = \begin{cases} 1 - \exp\big(\lambda_{\text{fast}} - \lambda_{\text{slow}}\big) & \text{if } \lambda_{\text{fast}} - \lambda_{\text{slow}} < -2.5 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}

Here \exp(\lambda_{\text{fast}} - \lambda_{\text{slow}}) is the ratio of the fast average to the slow one. The fraction jumps from 0 to about 0.92 at the trigger, since 1 - e^{-2.5} \approx 0.92, so a kidnap is answered at once. The benchmark's routine injection rate, 1.3% of particles per step when nothing is wrong, shows how rarely the trigger fires on its own.

The code replaces k particles chosen uniformly at random, without replacement, with fresh uniform draws over the free cells and headings, and it resets their weights to 1/N. The choice does not look at the weights. It is a partial Fisher–Yates shuffle: step i swaps position i with a uniform position j in [i, N), using the filter's seeded random stream, and the first k positions hold the replaced particles.39

The random choice matters because of systematic resampling. It leaves copies of one particle side by side, so replacing the first k indices would remove one or two hypotheses at once.

40 
function PARTICLE-STEP(particles, weights, rot, fwd, z):
    for each particle i:
        particle i ← MOTION(particle i, rot, fwd)   # each draws its own noise
        ℓ[i] ← sum over beams of log LIKELIHOOD(z, RAY-CAST(particle i))
    weights ← weights × exp(ℓ − max ℓ), normalised
    if ESS(weights) < N / 2:
        particles, weights ← SYSTEMATIC-RESAMPLE(particles, weights)
    if the fast average has fallen 2.5 below the slow one:
        replace a random subset of the particles with random poses over the floor
function PARTICLE-STEP(particles, weights, rot, fwd, z):
    σ_r ← SCALE × (0.05 + 0.15 × |rot|)
    σ_f ← SCALE × (0.03 + 0.10 × |fwd|)
    # 1. motion: two normals per particle, turn noise first, then drive noise
    for each particle i:
        r_i ← rot + σ_r × NORMAL()
        f_i ← fwd + σ_f × NORMAL()
        (x_i, y_i, θ_i) ← MOTION(walls, x_i, y_i, θ_i, r_i, f_i)
    # 2. sensing: each particle's own beams, then its log-likelihood
    for each particle i:
        for each beam j:
            e[i][j] ← RAY-CAST(walls, x_i, y_i, θ_i + 2π × j / R)
        ℓ[i] ← sum over j of log L(z_j | e[i][j])
    ℓ* ← max over i of ℓ[i]
    for each particle i:
        lik[i] ← exp(ℓ[i] − ℓ*)
    L̄ ← ℓ* + log(mean of lik)                       # log of the mean likelihood
    if this is the first tick:
        λ_slow ← L̄
        λ_fast ← L̄
    else:
        λ_slow ← λ_slow + 0.01 × (L̄ − λ_slow)
        λ_fast ← λ_fast + 0.1 × (L̄ − λ_fast)
    if AUGMENTED and λ_fast − λ_slow < −2.5:
        p ← 1 − exp(λ_fast − λ_slow)
    else:
        p ← 0
    w[i] ← w[i] × lik[i] for each particle, then w ← w / sum(w)
    ESS ← 1 / sum of w[i]²
    # 3. resampling: keep the heavy particles when the weights have concentrated
    if ESS < N / 2:
        SYSTEMATIC-RESAMPLE(particles, w)           # copies, then every w[i] = 1 / N
    # 4. recovery: replace k random particles with uniform random poses
    k ← round(p × N)
    if k > 0:
        perm ← (0, 1, ..., N − 1)
        for i in 0 to k − 1:                        # partial Fisher-Yates shuffle
            j ← i + min(N − 1 − i, floor(UNIFORM() × (N − i)))
            swap perm[i] and perm[j]
        for each i in the first k entries of perm:
            (x_i, y_i, θ_i) ← uniform over free cells and headings
            w[i] ← 1 / N
        w ← w / sum(w)
    return particles, w

The simple version has the four stages in order. The full version writes out the motion noise, the sensing, the averages and the injection, which follow the code line by line. Two details matter. The averages and the injection fraction are computed from the likelihoods before resampling. The shuffle draws its numbers after the resampling draws, so the Python and JavaScript versions draw the same numbers.

The estimate and its uncertainty

The page does not report the mean of all the particles. With two look-alike rooms, a mean of the two clusters falls between them, in a wall. The estimate is instead the densest cluster: the heaviest particle i^* and the particles close to it, within 1.5 cells and within 0.6 rad of its heading.41

42
M_i = \mathbf{1}\big[\,\mathrm{dist}(i, i^*) < 1.5\ \text{ and }\ |\operatorname{wrap}(\theta_i - \theta_{i^*})| < 0.6\,\big], \qquad \tilde w_i = \dfrac{w_i M_i}{\sum_j w_j M_j}

The cluster's share of the total weight is \sum_j w_j M_j, which the panel reports as its mass. The estimate is the weighted average over the cluster. The heading is averaged as a circle, so that headings near plus and minus pi do not cancel.

43
\hat x = \sum_i \tilde w_i\, x_i, \qquad \hat y = \sum_i \tilde w_i\, y_i, \qquad \hat\theta = \operatorname{atan2}\Big(\sum_i \tilde w_i \sin\theta_i,\ \sum_i \tilde w_i \cos\theta_i\Big)

The uncertainty is the covariance of the cluster's positions around (\hat x, \hat y).44 The page draws an ellipse whose radii are twice the square roots of the covariance's eigenvalues, which is the 2-sigma region.45

44
\Sigma = \sum_i \tilde w_i \begin{pmatrix} (x_i - \hat x)^2 & (x_i - \hat x)(y_i - \hat y) \\ (x_i - \hat x)(y_i - \hat y) & (y_i - \hat y)^2 \end{pmatrix}

The grid filter has no cluster. Its estimate is the most probable bin, as described above.

On each tick the page moves the true robot, then gives both filters the commanded turn and drive and the new beams. The grid filter predicts and then updates, and the particle filter runs its four stages. At the start, both filters take one update with zero motion so that the first beams are used.46

The place count written to the event log is a rough measure of how many rooms the belief has split between. It sums the particles' weight into 1-cell bins, then counts the groups of bins that touch along an edge and each hold at least 2% of the total weight.4748

Random numbers: mulberry32

Every random draw comes from mulberry32, a small seeded generator. The same seed gives the same floor, noise and beams in Python and in JavaScript, so the page, the command line and the tests draw the same numbers. The generator is written as a counter. After k draws its state is the seed plus k times a constant, and each output is a fixed mixing function of that state. The k-th number can therefore be computed directly, and a batch of thousands is one vector expression.49

5051
u_k = \dfrac{\operatorname{mix}\big((s + C\,k) \bmod 2^{32}\big)}{2^{32}}, \qquad C = \mathtt{0x6D2B79F5}, \qquad k = 1, 2, \dots

Here s is the seed and \operatorname{mix} is the mulberry32 output mixing, which xor-shifts and multiplies the 32-bit state. Normal draws use the Marsaglia polar method. Two uniforms are mapped to x = 2u_1 - 1 and y = 2u_2 - 1. The pair is rejected unless 0 < s < 1, where s = x^2 + y^2. An accepted pair gives one normal, z = x\sqrt{-2 \ln s / s}, and the second value of the pair is not used.52

Results and parameters

How these were measured: 16 seeded trials (2 floors, 4 floor seeds, 2 robot seeds), 100 steps each, in Python on one core; the full table is in results/localize_benchmark.md53. A trial succeeds when the estimate stays within 0.5 m for ten steps in a row. With 2000 particles that happens in 14 of 16 trials, at a median of 2 steps (the first step of the held window). With 500 particles it fails in 6 of 16, and with 100 in 12 of 16: the first scan leaves too few guesses, and they all sit in one of the look-alike rooms. The grid filter fails in none, at 14 ms per step against 6 ms for the particles (timings from a quiet run; they move with machine load, the ratio less so). The sensor and odometry sweeps move the failure count by a few trials either way, within what 16 trials can resolve, so they are not a trend.

After a kidnap (at step 40), augmented MCL re-finds the robot in a median of 11 steps, with 2 of 16 trials never recovering. Plain MCL, without the injection, recovers in only 2 of 16. Augmented MCL re-scatters 1.3% of particles per step when nothing is wrong, which is the false-alarm rate, and 13% per step after the kidnap. The grid filter takes a median of 33 steps: its belief stays confident in the old room until enough new beams outweigh it. The 60-step window is short.

HOW IT WORKS

Two filters, the same robot, the same beams. Both start from total ignorance. On 16 seeded trials, the 2000-particle filter holds within 0.5 m for ten steps in 15 of them; the exact grid filter does so in all 16, but takes about 2.7 times as long per step in this implementation. Two devices do most of the work: the particles are resampled when their weights bunch up, and a fraction is re-scattered over the floor when the beams stop matching the belief.

1. Bayes' rule: belief times evidence

New to this? Start with a guess about where the robot could be, the prior. Each sensor reading says how likely it is under each place. Multiply the two, divide by their total, and the result is the posterior: the new belief. The robot is never "found" in one jump; the belief shifts a little with each reading.

Written out: p(x | z) = p(z | x) p(x) / p(z). Here x is the pose (position and heading), z the beams, and p(z) just the sum that makes the numbers add to one.

2. Predict, then update

New to this? Each tick has two steps. Predict: move every possible pose by the odometry, spreading the belief because the wheels drift. Update: multiply by how well the beams fit each pose, then renormalise. Prediction makes the belief wider; the sensor makes it narrower.

With no sensor the belief only spreads out. With no odometry the robot could only ever be where the first scan put it. The two together give the filter its shape.

3. Grid filter: one number per bin

New to this? Chop the floor into small bins, each with a heading, and keep one probability for every bin. Prediction moves probability from each bin to where the odometry could have taken it. Update multiplies by the likelihood of the beams in that bin. Nothing is sampled, so the result is the exact Bayes filter on these bins.

The cost grows with the number of bins, so it is exact but limited: 48 by 32 positions with 16 headings is 24 576 bins, and every bin is updated on every tick.

4. Particle filter: a sample of the belief

New to this? Instead of a number per bin, keep a few thousand guesses, each a pose. Predict moves each guess by the odometry plus its own random noise. Update scores each guess by how well its own beams match the sensor, and that score becomes its weight. The cloud of weighted guesses is the belief.

The cost grows with the particle count, not the size of the floor, which is why it scales to large maps. The price is randomness: fewer particles can lose a place for good.

5. Resampling: keep the good guesses

New to this? After a few updates most weight sits on a handful of particles. Resampling redraws the cloud in proportion to the weights: heavy particles are copied, light ones die. The effective sample size (ESS) measures how concentrated the weights are; when it falls below half the particles, the cloud is resampled. Systematic resampling uses one random offset and evenly spaced pointers, so every copy count is as even as it can be.

6. Kidnapped robot: inject new guesses

New to this? If someone carries the robot to a new room, every particle is now wrong and the cloud can never recover by itself. The filter compares the average likelihood of the beams now with its longer-run average. When the recent average falls well below the long-run one, it replaces the lowest-weight particles with random poses over the whole floor. This is augmented Monte Carlo localisation.

How these were measured: 16 seeded trials (2 floors, 4 floor seeds, 2 robot seeds), 100 steps each, in Python on one core; the full table is results/localize_benchmark.md. A trial succeeds when the estimate stays within 0.5 m for ten steps in a row. With 2000 particles that happens in 15 of 16 trials, at a median of 2 steps (the first step of the held window). With 500 particles it fails in 4 of 16, and with 100 in 11 of 16: the first scan leaves too few guesses, and they all sit in one of the look-alike rooms. The grid filter fails in none, at 40 ms per step against 15 ms for the particles (timings from a quiet run; they move with machine load, the ratio less so). The sensor and odometry sweeps move the failure count by a few trials either way, within what 16 trials can resolve, so they are not a trend.

After a kidnap (at step 40), augmented MCL re-finds the robot in a median of 13 steps, with 3 of 16 trials never recovering. Plain MCL, without the injection, recovers in only 2 of 16. Augmented MCL re-scatters 1.2% of particles per step when nothing is wrong, which is the false-alarm rate, and 19% per step after the kidnap. The grid filter takes a median of 33 steps: its belief stays confident in the old room until enough new beams outweigh it. The 60-step window is short. In a separate spot check of six kidnaps with a 240-step window, five recovered, after 52 to 167 steps, and one stayed lost: the injected particles are a fresh global search, and the look-alike rooms can hold it for a while.

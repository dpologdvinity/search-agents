HIDDEN MARKOV MODELS

GHOST HUNT

The ghosts are invisible. The sonar reports only how far away each one is, and every ping is noisy. Bust a ghost by firing at the cell it is standing in.
STATUS LOADING TURN 0 BUSTED 0/0

HOW TO PLAY

The ghosts hide somewhere in a maze, and you start in the top-left room. Each turn you move one cell or wait. Then the ghosts move, and the sonar pings you with each ghost's distance.

To bust a ghost, turn on BUST MODE and click a neighbouring cell or your own cell. A bust removes a ghost only if it is standing in that cell at that moment. The fog shows where each ghost probably is, and the game ends when every ghost is busted.

Press AUTOPILOT to let the hunter play. It follows the fog and busts when it is sure. After a bust, VITERBI REPLAY shows the most likely path the busted ghost took.

Hover or click a cell next to you to move or bust.

HOW IT WORKS

A ghost is a hidden Markov model (HMM): its true state, its cell and heading, changes by a known motion rule, and the sonar shows a noisy function of that state. The state is hidden, so the hunter keeps a belief, a probability for each state, and updates it every turn. This page runs the exact forward recursion, or a particle filter with a chosen number of samples, in the browser. Viterbi then recovers the most likely path after a bust.

The maze, the ghosts and the turn

The maze is a perfect maze: an odd grid of rooms and passages in which there is exactly one route between any two open cells. It is carved by a seeded backtracker, which walks from room to room, knocks down the wall between the current room and a random unvisited room two steps away, and backtracks when it is stuck. Every room is reached once, so the result is a tree.1 The player starts in the top-left room. Because the route is unique, the sonar distance is the length of the corridor path, not a straight-line guess.

The open cells are numbered 0 to K−1, and each one has up to four neighbours, in the order north, east, south, west. The distance dist(a, b) is the number of steps along the corridors between two open cells, found by a breadth-first search from every cell.2

A hidden state is s = 4k + h, where k is the open cell and h is the heading of the ghost's last move, 0 to 3 for north, east, south and west. The heading is in the state so that the patrol model can follow a corridor. There are 4K states. A 15 by 15 maze has 97 open cells, so 388 states.3

Each ghost starts in a random state chosen from the eligible states, those whose cell is at least 4 steps from the player. The prior, the belief before any ping, is uniform over the eligible states and zero elsewhere. Every filter starts from this same prior.4

5
\pi(s) = \begin{cases} 1 / |E| & \text{if } s \in E \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}, \qquad E = \{\, 4k + h : \mathrm{dist}(k, \mathrm{start}) \ge 4 \,\}

A turn has a fixed order. The player acts first: it moves, waits or fires a bust. A bust resolves against the ghosts' current cells, before they move, and it does not move the player. Then each live ghost takes one motion step with the player's new cell, and the sonar pings the distance from that cell to the ghost. A ghost busted this turn gives no ping. The filters follow the same order.6

Each live ghost draws two random numbers per turn, one for its move and one for its ping, from its own stream. Two runs with the same seed therefore see the same ghost walks and pings even when the player plays differently. The benchmark uses this common-random-numbers design, so the filters are compared on the same ghosts.7

Every ping is labelled with the ghost it measures, and the ghosts move independently. The joint belief over all the ghosts therefore factorises, so each ghost gets its own filter, and the per-ghost beliefs are exact without a joint filter. Correlations between ghosts would appear only if the sonar could not tell them apart.8

The three ghost motions

A motion model is the transition probability P(s' | s, p): the chance that a ghost in state s moves to s' when the player stands in cell p. The three models are the random walk, the lurker and the patrol. The hunter knows every probability, because the models are known.3

The random walk stays put with probability 0.1. Otherwise it moves to one of its m open neighbours, chosen uniformly.9

9
P(s' \mid s) = \begin{cases} 0.1 & \text{if } s' = s \text{ (stay)} \\ 0.9 / m & \text{for each open neighbour of the cell} \end{cases}

The lurker drifts toward the player. Staying and each open neighbour are weighted by exp(−β · distance to the player), with β = 1, and the weights are normalised. A cell nearer the player gets more weight, but no move is certain.10

10
P(s' \mid s, p) = \dfrac{\exp\big(-\beta\,\mathrm{dist}(k', p)\big)}{\sum_{s''} \exp\big(-\beta\,\mathrm{dist}(k'', p)\big)}, \qquad \beta = 1

Here k' is the cell of s', and the sum runs over staying and each open neighbour of the current cell. The player's cell p is known, so the transition is known too, and the forward algorithm can use it.

The patrol keeps its heading when the corridor continues. The code's rule has three cases. At a junction with a side opening, it goes straight with probability 0.8, and the side openings share the other 0.2. In a straight corridor with no side opening, it goes on with 0.8 or turns back with 0.2. At a dead end it turns back. It never waits.11

11 
function PATROL-MOVES(cell, heading):
    if the way ahead is open:
        if there is a side opening:
            ahead with probability 0.8; each side opening shares 0.2
        else:
            ahead with probability 0.8; back with probability 0.2
    else if there is a side opening:
        each side opening with equal probability
    else:
        turn back                              # dead end
function PATROL-MOVES(cell, heading):
    open ← the open directions of cell
    back ← (heading + 2) mod 4
    fwd-ok ← heading is in open
    turns ← open directions other than heading and back
    back-ok ← back is in open
    out ← empty list
    if fwd-ok:
        if turns is not empty:                 # junction
            add (ahead, 0.8)
            for each d in turns:
                add (side d, 0.2 / |turns|)
        else if back-ok:                       # straight corridor
            add (ahead, 0.8)
            add (back, 0.2)
        else:
            add (ahead, 1.0)
    else if turns is not empty:                # only if the start heading faces a wall
        for each d in turns:
            add (side d, 1 / |turns|)
    else if back-ok:                           # dead end: turn around
        add (back, 1.0)
    else:
        add (stay, 1.0)                        # isolated cell: cannot happen in a perfect maze
    return out

The full version follows the code. The "ahead" move keeps the heading, and a side move sets the heading to the side it takes. The branch where the way ahead is a wall can only occur with an arbitrary starting heading, since the heading after any move points into an open cell. Every branch returns probabilities that sum to one.

The sonar

The ping reports a whole number of cells, from 0 to R, where R is the largest distance in the maze, the diameter. The reading is drawn from a discrete Gaussian centred on the true distance and normalised over the readings the sonar can show. Its standard deviation σ is 0.5, 1.0 or 2.0 cells, and 1.0 by default.12

13
P(r \mid d) = \max\!\Big(\dfrac{\exp\!\big(-\tfrac{(r-d)^2}{2\sigma^2}\big)}{Z(d)},\ 10^{-300}\Big), \qquad Z(d) = \sum_{r'=0}^{R} \exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{(r'-d)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)

Here d is the true maze distance from the player to the ghost, r is the reading, and Z(d) makes each row a distribution. The floor at 10^{-300} keeps a far reading from underflowing to exactly zero, since a zero would make the logarithm in Viterbi undefined. The table has one row per distance and is built once per game. The emission P(r \mid d) is the only place the sensor enters the filters.13

To draw a reading, the game inverts the row's cumulative distribution at a uniform random number u. It returns the first reading whose running total passes u.14

The forward algorithm: exact filtering

Filtering means computing the belief over the hidden state at the current turn, given every reading so far. The forward algorithm does this exactly for a hidden Markov model. At each turn it runs two steps. The predict step pushes every state's probability through the transition model. The update step multiplies each state by the likelihood of the reading if the ghost were there, then normalises.15

16
b'(s') = \sum_{s} b(s)\, P(s' \mid s, p_t), \qquad b(s') \leftarrow \dfrac{b'(s')\, P(r_t \mid \mathrm{dist}(k(s'), p_t))}{\sum_{s''} b'(s'')\, P(r_t \mid \mathrm{dist}(k(s''), p_t))}

Here b_t(s) is the belief that the ghost is in state s at turn t, p_t is the player's cell, and r_t is the reading. At turn 0 there is no predict, since the prior already describes time 0, so only the update runs. The belief summed over headings gives the probability of each cell, and the hunter uses that marginal.17

Example. The state space is small enough to keep. A 15 by 15 maze has 388 states, and each state has at most five successors (stay and four moves), so one predict costs at most 5 × 388 multiplications per ghost.

16 
function FORWARD-STEP(belief, player, reading):
    belief ← PREDICT(belief, player)            # push the belief through the motion model
    for each state s:
        belief[s] ← belief[s] × SONAR(reading, distance of s from player)
    return belief / sum(belief)
function FORWARD-STEP(belief, player, reading, first-turn):
    if not first-turn:
        trans ← MOTION-TABLE(model, player)     # successors and probabilities of every state
        new ← zeros over all states
        for each state s with belief[s] > 0:
            for each (s2, q) in trans[s]:
                new[s2] ← new[s2] + belief[s] × q
        belief ← new
    total ← 0
    for each state s:
        belief[s] ← belief[s] × emis[dist(cell(s), player)][reading]
        total ← total + belief[s]
    if total > 0:
        belief ← belief / total
    else:                                       # numerical guard: every state underflowed
        for each state s:
            belief[s] ← emis[dist(cell(s), player)][reading]
        belief ← belief / sum(belief)
    return belief

The full version differs from the simple one in two ways. The first turn skips the predict step, since the prior already describes time 0. The guard handles the rare case where every state underflows to zero, and then the belief is set from the sonar alone.

Particle filtering: a sample of the belief

When the state space is too large to keep, a particle filter keeps N particles, each one a sampled state, and the weighted histogram of the particles stands in for the belief. The page uses it when the particle filter is chosen, with 10 to 1000 particles per ghost, 300 by default.18

The particles start as draws from the prior. Each draw inverts the cumulative prior at a fresh uniform number. Each turn, every particle moves by one sample of the motion model. A particle whose motion has only one option moves without drawing a random number.19

The update multiplies each particle's weight by the sonar likelihood of its distance, then normalises. The effective sample size (ESS) is 1 / Σw², which equals N when the weights are equal and falls toward 1 when one particle holds almost all the weight. When ESS falls below N/2, the particles are resampled.20

20
w_i \leftarrow \dfrac{w_i\, P(r_t \mid \mathrm{dist}(k_i, p_t))}{\sum_j w_j\, P(r_t \mid \mathrm{dist}(k_j, p_t))}, \qquad \mathrm{ESS} = \dfrac{1}{\sum_i w_i^2}, \qquad \text{resample when } \mathrm{ESS} < N/2

Here k_i is the cell of particle i. The resampling is systematic. It draws one uniform offset, then places N evenly spaced pointers over the cumulative weights, so each particle is copied about N times its weight. The copies are spread as evenly as the weights allow.21

21
u_0 = \dfrac{U}{N}, \qquad p_i = u_0 + \dfrac{i}{N}, \qquad j_i = \text{the first } j \text{ with } C_j > p_i, \qquad C_j = \sum_{k \le j} w_k

Here U is one uniform draw on [0, 1), and the search returns the last index if rounding leaves no cumulative value above the pointer. The filter's belief is the histogram of the particles' cells, weighted by their weights.22

23 
function PARTICLE-STEP(particles, weights, player, reading):
    for each particle: move it by one sample of the motion model from player
    for each particle: weights ← weights × SONAR(reading, distance of particle from player)
    normalise weights
    if ESS(weights) < N / 2:
        particles, weights ← SYSTEMATIC-RESAMPLE(particles, weights)
function PARTICLE-STEP(particles, weights, player, reading):
    trans ← MOTION-TABLE(model, player)
    for each particle i in state s:
        options ← trans[s]
        if options has one entry:
            particle i ← that entry's state     # a forced move draws nothing
            continue
        u ← UNIFORM()
        acc ← 0
        chosen ← last state in options          # rounding fallback
        for each (s2, q) in options:
            acc ← acc + q
            if u < acc:
                chosen ← s2
                stop
        particle i ← chosen
    for each particle i:
        w[i] ← w[i] × emis[dist(cell(particle i), player)][reading]
    total ← sum(w)
    if total > 0:
        w ← w / total
    else:                                       # every particle underflowed
        w[i] ← 1 / N for each i
    ESS ← 1 / sum of w[i]²
    if ESS < N / 2:
        SYSTEMATIC-RESAMPLE(particles, w)       # copies, then every weight is 1 / N

The simple version shows the cycle of move, weigh and resample. The full version adds the details that matter for matching the code: the forced-move shortcut draws no number, the choice of successor is the first option whose running total exceeds the uniform number, and the all-zero guard resets the weights.

Viterbi: the most likely path

The belief answers "where is the ghost now", using the readings so far. Viterbi answers a different question: which whole sequence of states explains all the readings best, including readings after a turn. It is the same recursion as the forward algorithm with a maximum in place of the sum, plus back-pointers that record which predecessor won each step, so the path can be read off afterwards.24

The recursion works in log space, so products of small numbers become sums. Let δt(s) be the best log-probability of any state sequence that ends in s at turn t.

25
\delta_0(s) = \log \pi(s) + \log L_0(s), \qquad \delta_t(s') = \max_{s}\big[\delta_{t-1}(s) + \log P(s' \mid s, p_t)\big] + \log L_t(s'), \qquad \psi_t(s') = \arg\max_{s}\big[\delta_{t-1}(s) + \log P(s' \mid s, p_t)\big]

Here L_t(s) = P(r_t \mid \mathrm{dist}(k(s), p_t)) is the sonar likelihood. The code keeps the lowest-index predecessor when two candidates tie, so the path is the same on every run. States that cannot occur, and states with zero prior, are minus infinity and never win.26

27
s_T^{*} = \arg\max_s \delta_T(s), \qquad s_{t-1}^{*} = \psi_t(s_t^{*})

The path is read backwards from the best final state, following the back-pointers. A busted ghost's whole path is observed, so the page can replay it against the true path.

24 
function VITERBI(prior, players, readings):
    delta[s] ← log(prior[s]) + log(SONAR(readings[0], s, players[0])) for each state
    for t in 1 to T − 1:
        for each state s2:
            delta'[s2] ← max over s of (delta[s] + log P(s2 | s, players[t]))
            back[t][s2] ← the s that gave the maximum
            delta'[s2] ← delta'[s2] + log SONAR(readings[t], s2, players[t])
        delta ← delta'
    path ← the state with the largest delta, then follow back from the last turn
function VITERBI(prior, players, readings):
    NEG ← −infinity
    for each state s:
        if prior[s] > 0:
            delta[s] ← log(prior[s]) + log(emis[dist(cell(s), players[0])][readings[0]])
        else:
            delta[s] ← NEG
    for t in 1 to T − 1:
        trans ← MOTION-TABLE(model, players[t])
        new[s2] ← NEG and ptr[s2] ← −1 for every state s2
        for each state s with delta[s] > NEG:
            for each (s2, q) in trans[s]:
                cand ← delta[s] + log(q)
                if cand > new[s2]:              # strict: the lowest-index predecessor wins a tie
                    new[s2] ← cand
                    ptr[s2] ← s
        for each state s2 with new[s2] > NEG:
            new[s2] ← new[s2] + log(emis[dist(cell(s2), players[t])][readings[t]])
        delta ← new
        back[t] ← ptr
    last ← the state with the largest delta (lowest index on ties)
    path ← [last]
    for t from T − 1 down to 1:
        last ← back[t][last]
        path ← [last] followed by path
    return path

The simple version has the same shape as the full one: score every path, keep the best predecessor at each turn, then read the path back. The full version sets zero-prior states to minus infinity, and it applies the strict comparison that breaks ties toward the lowest index.

On the page, a busted ghost's Viterbi path is drawn in cyan next to its true path in pink. The benchmark compares the Viterbi cell at each turn with the filtered MAP cell, which is the most likely cell from the readings so far only.28

The autopilot: busting when sure

The autopilot is a simple policy that reads only the beliefs, so it works with either filter. Each turn it takes the most probable cell over all live ghosts, the most confident peak. If that peak holds more than half of the ghost's probability and is the player's cell or a neighbour, it fires there. Otherwise it walks one step along a shortest corridor path toward the peak. If the player already stands on the peak, it walks toward the second-best cell, because sonar from another corridor sharpens the belief.29

30 
function AUTOPILOT(game):
    peak ← the most probable cell over all live ghosts, with its probability
    if the peak probability > 0.5 and the peak is the player's cell or a neighbour:
        fire at the peak
    else:
        step toward the peak along a shortest corridor path
        (if standing on the peak, step toward the second-best cell)
function AUTOPILOT(game):
    best ← none
    for each live ghost g:
        marg ← the belief over cells for g
        k ← ARGMAX(marg)                         # lowest index on ties
        if best is none or marg[k] > best.conf:
            best ← (marg[k], k, marg)
    if best is none:
        return wait
    conf, k, marg ← best
    if conf > 0.5:
        if k = player cell:
            return bust here                     # "b."
        d ← FIRST-STEP(player cell, k)
        if d ≥ 0 and the neighbour in direction d is k:
            return bust d                        # "bN", "bE", "bS" or "bW"
    if k = player cell:
        k ← ARGMAX(marg, skip = player cell)     # the second peak
    d ← FIRST-STEP(player cell, k)
    if d < 0:
        return wait
    return move d
function FIRST-STEP(p, target):
    for each direction d in N, E, S, W:
        q ← the neighbour of p in direction d
        if q is open and dist(q, target) = dist(p, target) − 1:
            return d
    return −1

The threshold is the constant BUST_THRESHOLD, 0.5. The autopilot is a simple rule, not an optimal hunter, so its turn counts measure the belief and this policy together.

The simple version gives the rule. The full version adds the second-peak fallback and the first-step function, which breaks ties by the fixed order north, east, south, west.31

Results and parameters

These come from the seeded benchmark (python -m ghosthunt benchmark), one core in Python. Games are 12 per row, so differences of a few turns are noise. Ghost-cell mass for the particle filter is lower than the exact filter at 10 to 50 particles: the likely reason is that a small crowd collapses onto one corridor and loses the other, which is consistent with those rows busting fewer games. The autopilot is a simple rule, not an optimal hunter, so the turn counts measure the belief plus this policy together. Pings give only distances, so every cell at the same distance from the player looks the same to one ping, and the belief sharpens only as the player moves and listens from other corridors.

The page runs the same rules in JavaScript, in web/js/ghosthunt-core.js32, and the parity tests check that the two agree. The full table is in results/ghosthunt_benchmark.md33. The benchmark has three experiments. The play runs full autopilot games with the exact filter and with particle filters, and the Viterbi block scores the exact-filter games' busted ghosts. The convergence block replays the recorded pings through the particle filters of growing N.

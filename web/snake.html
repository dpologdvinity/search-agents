HOW IT WORKS

A snake on a 12 × 12 board steers by choosing one of three actions each step. Its goal is to eat apples, and each apple adds a segment to its body. The game ends if it runs into a wall or its own body, or if it goes too long without food.

Two learned agents steer the snake, and both are shown here. The default is an evaluation function: it scores each safe move by a weighted sum of eight numbers computed from the position the move leads to, and its eight weights were tuned by the cross-entropy method. The other is a neural net, a function built from weighted sums, with 339 weights bred by a genetic algorithm. Two classic baselines, greedy and a breadth-first search planner, are measured on the same boards for comparison.

The game and its rules

The board is a 12 × 12 grid of cells, each addressed by coordinates (x, y), where x is the column and y the row, growing downward. The snake is a list of segments. The first is its head and the last its tail. It starts in the middle of the board with three segments at (6, 6), (5, 6) and (4, 6), heading east. A heading is one of north, east, south or west, numbered 0 to 3 in clockwise order.

Each step the snake takes one action: left, straight or right, measured from its heading. The actions are relative, so the policy never needs compass directions. A left or right action turns the heading by a quarter turn, and straight leaves it alone. The snake cannot reverse, so these three actions are the only choices1.

h' = (h + \delta_a) \bmod 4, \qquad \delta_{\text{left}} = -1,\quad \delta_{\text{straight}} = 0,\quad \delta_{\text{right}} = +1
123

Here h is the heading before the step and h' the heading after it, and \delta_a is the quarter turn of action a. Adding 1 turns clockwise, and the modulo keeps the result in 0 to 33. Example. In a new game the head is at (6, 6) heading east. Straight enters (7, 6), and left turns the head north and enters (6, 5).

The head then moves into the cell ahead. The move ends the game if that cell is outside the board (a wall death) or holds a body cell (a body death). The tail is treated with care. If the snake does not eat, the tail leaves its cell in the same step, so the tail's cell can be entered. If the snake eats, the tail stays in place and blocks its cell45.

Eating adds one apple to the score and one segment to the body, and the food moves to a uniformly random empty cell. Each empty cell is equally likely, so with |E| empty cells each has probability 1/|E|6. An idle counter counts the steps since the last apple. The game ends as starved when the counter reaches the cap of two laps of the board, 2N^2 = 288 steps. Filling the whole board ends the game as full, the only win78.

\text{starved} \iff \text{idle} \ge 2N^2 = 2 \cdot 12^2 = 288
78

Here N = 12 is the side of the board. The cap stops a looping snake from playing forever, and it is the same for every agent in the benchmark below.

The score of one game

Both learned agents are trained on one number per game, the game score, which is also called fitness when it is used to rank candidates. A game that does not end by starvation scores its apples, plus a small survival bonus of at most 0.25 for lasting up to 300 steps. Beyond 300 steps each extra step costs 0.0002, so drifting for thousands of moves earns no bonus and pays a small cost. A starved game is penalised instead: it keeps its apples, loses 0.5, and gets no survival bonus, so looping is worse than dying cleanly910.

s(g) = \begin{cases} \text{apples} - 0.5 & \text{if starved} \\ \text{apples} + 0.25\,\dfrac{\min(\text{steps},\,300)}{300} - 0.0002\,\max(0,\ \text{steps} - 300) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
10

Here s(g) is the score of game g. The constants are the survival cap of 300 steps, the survival bonus of 0.25, the step cost of 0.0002 and the starvation penalty of 0.5, all set in the code11. A candidate's fitness is the mean of this score over the boards it played.

The default agent: an evaluation function

The default agent is an evaluation function, a rule that gives each position a number. For each move that does not end the game, it imagines the resulting position, computes eight features (numbers between 0 and 1 read from that position), and takes the move whose weighted sum of features is largest. A weight is the number that multiplies one feature. The features and the rules are hand-built, and the eight weights are the only part that was learned12.

A move is first applied in imagination. Let h' be the new head, and B' the new body: the new head followed by the old body, with the old tail removed unless the snake ate. The last cell of B' is the new tail t'. Every feature comes from one breadth-first search (BFS) that starts at h'. A BFS visits cells in order of their distance in steps, so the first time it reaches a cell it has the shortest route there. This search runs over empty cells and gives three values at once: the distance d_f in steps from the head to the food, the number R of empty cells it reaches, and whether it reaches a cell next to the tail. The tail t' is a goal the search may touch but not pass through, because it moves away on the next step13.

\begin{aligned} S(a) &= \sum_{k=1}^{8} w_k\, f_k(a) \\ a^\ast &= \operatorname{argmax}_{a \in A_{\text{safe}}} S(a) \end{aligned}
14

Here f_k(a) is feature k of the position after move a, w_k its weight, and A_{\text{safe}} the moves that do not end the game. If A_{\text{safe}} is empty, the snake goes straight. Ties keep the earlier move in the order straight, left, right, because a later move must score strictly higher to replace it15.

\begin{aligned} f_1 &= \mathbf{1}[\text{eats}] \\ f_2 &= \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if eats} \\ \max\!\left(0,\ 1 - \dfrac{d_f}{2N}\right) & \text{if the food is reachable} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \\ f_3 &= \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if eats or the food is reachable} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \\ f_4 &= \begin{cases} R\,/\,(N^2 - |B'|) & \text{if } N^2 > |B'| \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \\ f_5 &= \min\!\left(1,\ R\,/\,|B'|\right) \\ f_6 &= \mathbf{1}[\text{a reached cell is next to } t'] \\ f_7 &= \tfrac{1}{4}\,\bigl|\{\text{neighbours of } h' \text{ on the board that are not in } B'\}\bigr| \\ f_8 &= |B'|\,/\,N^2 \end{aligned}
161718

Here N = 12, so 2N = 24 and N^2 = 144. The food features use d_f, and R is the number of empty cells the search reaches, not counting the head. The room feature f_5 divides by the body length |B'|, and the area feature f_4 divides by the number of empty cells on the board after the move. f_7 counts the head's neighbours that are on the board and free, and the new tail counts as occupied19.

The committed weights are in snake/data/evaluator.json20. They were learned by the cross-entropy method described below.

The tail-reachable weight is the largest. The two food terms together need a closer look. For food that is reachable and not eaten, they add 0.8261\,(1 - d_f/24) - 0.6941 = 0.1320 - 0.0344\,d_f. That is positive for food within 3 steps, and negative beyond it. Beyond 24 steps it is the full -0.6941. So from the food terms alone, reachable food farther than about 3 steps scores below food that cannot be reached at all, which scores 0. The tuner found this combination; the page does not claim it as a design goal. The other features, and the apple term, shape the choice as well.

Example. A move that eats has f_1 = f_2 = f_3 = 1. Its apple and food terms add 0.7266 + 0.8261 - 0.6941 = 0.8586 to its score, before the area, room, tail, exits and length terms are added.

function CHOOSE(game, weights):
    best ← none
    for each action a in (straight, left, right):    # ties keep the earlier action
        if DIES(game, a):
            continue                                 # a move that ends the game is never chosen
        score ← sum over k of weights[k] · FEATURES(game, a)[k]
        if best is none or score > best.score:
            best ← (a, score)
    if best is none:
        return straight                              # every move ends the game
    return best.action
function CHOOSE(game, weights):
    best ← none
    for each action a in (straight, left, right):    # ties keep the earlier action
        if DIES(game, a):
            continue                                 # a move that ends the game is never chosen
        f ← FEATURES(game, a)
        score ← weights[1]·f[1] + weights[2]·f[2] + ... + weights[8]·f[8]
        if best is none or score > best.score:
            best ← (a, score)
    if best is none:
        return straight                              # every move ends the game
    return best.action

function DIES(game, a):
    cell ← NEXT-CELL(game, a)                        # where the head would go
    if cell is outside the board:
        return true
    if cell = game.food:                             # eating keeps the whole body in place
        return cell is in game.body
    return cell is in game.body without its last cell  # the tail leaves in the same step

function FEATURES(game, a):
    head ← NEXT-CELL(game, a)
    eats ← head = game.food
    if eats:
        body ← [head] + game.body                    # the tail stays
    else:
        body ← [head] + game.body without its last cell
    tail ← last cell of body
    blocked ← body without its last cell             # the tail may be reached, not passed
    n_empty ← 12 · 12 − |body|
    dist ← {head: 0};  queue ← [head];  tail_ok ← false
    while queue is not empty:                        # breadth-first search over empty cells
        cell ← first cell of queue;  remove it from queue
        for each neighbour c of cell, in order north, east, south, west:
            if c is outside the board or c in dist:
                continue
            if c = tail:
                tail_ok ← true
                continue
            if c in blocked:
                continue
            dist[c] ← dist[cell] + 1
            append c to queue
    reach ← |dist| − 1                               # reached empty cells, not the head
    if eats:
        food_near ← 1;  food_reach ← 1
    else if game.food in dist:
        food_near ← max(0, 1 − dist[game.food] / 24);  food_reach ← 1
    else:
        food_near ← 0;  food_reach ← 0
    exits ← 0
    for each neighbour c of head:
        if c is inside the board and c not in body:
            exits ← exits + 1
    return [
        1 if eats else 0,
        food_near,
        food_reach,
        reach / n_empty if n_empty > 0 else 0,
        min(1, reach / |body|),
        1 if tail_ok else 0,
        exits / 4,
        |body| / 144,
    ]

The search runs once and feeds five features: the food distance, the area, the room, the tail test and the food reachability. The tail is a goal, so the escape check can succeed at the tail without the search continuing through it. Distances are counted in whole steps and the food feature is scaled by 2N = 24, so food 24 steps away counts as 0 for f_2, while food on a reachable path still counts for f_3.

The order of the neighbours does not change any feature, since a BFS gives the same distances and reach in any order. The order matters only for the choice, through the tie rule.

Learning the weights: the cross-entropy method

The cross-entropy method (CEM) learns parameters by sampling. It keeps a Gaussian distribution, a bell curve with a mean and a standard deviation, for each parameter. Each generation it draws a population of candidate parameter vectors from that distribution, scores each candidate, keeps the best few (the elites), and moves the distribution toward them. Here the vector has eight entries, one per weight, and each weight has its own Gaussian23.

Each generation draws 48 candidates. Each candidate plays the same 6 boards, whose seeds are drawn anew each generation from 1000 up to 39,999, and its fitness is the mean game score over them. The 12 best are the elites. The Gaussian is then refit to them: its mean becomes the elites' mean, and its standard deviation becomes the elites' spread, floored at 0.05 so that the search never collapses to a single point2425.

\begin{aligned} \theta^{(i)} &= \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon^{(i)}, \qquad \varepsilon^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \\ F(\theta) &= \frac{1}{G} \sum_{g=1}^{G} s_g(\theta) \\ \mu' &= \frac{1}{K} \sum_{i \in E} \theta^{(i)} \\ \sigma'_j &= \max\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{K} \sum_{i \in E} \bigl(\theta^{(i)}_j - \mu'_j\bigr)^2},\ 0.05\right) \end{aligned}
262725

Here \mu and \sigma are the mean and standard deviation vectors, \odot multiplies each weight by its own standard deviation, and \varepsilon^{(i)} is a standard normal draw. G = 6 is the number of boards, s_g(\theta) the game score of board g, and E the set of K = 12 elites out of 48 candidates. The start is \mu = 0 and \sigma = 1.0 for every weight. The standard deviation divides by K, which is numpy's default, not K - 1.

The champion is chosen on fixed boards, not on the training fitness, since the training boards change each generation. The best candidate of any generation and the top five of the last generation are rounded to four decimals. Each is scored on 32 validation seeds, 40000 to 40031, which lie above the training range and below the benchmark's 50000, and the highest validation score is committed28. The committed run used 30 generations. Its champion came from generation 30, and scored a mean of 96.344 apples and 2,777 steps per game on those 32 boards20.

function CROSS-ENTROPY(generations):
    mean ← zeros(8);  sigma ← 1.0 for every weight
    repeat generations times:
        population ← 48 samples from Normal(mean, sigma)
        score each sample by its mean game score on 6 new boards
        elites ← the 12 best samples
        mean ← average of the elites
        sigma ← max(spread of the elites, 0.05)
    return the candidate that scores best on the validation boards

function PLAY(θ, seed):
    game ← new game with food from seed
    while game is alive:
        step game with CHOOSE(game, θ)
    return game

The neural net and its 17 senses

The neural net maps 17 numbers, its senses, to 3 numbers, one score per action. It is a function built from weighted sums. The snake takes the action with the largest score. The senses are read in the snake's own frame: ahead is the direction of the heading, and left and right are a quarter turn either side. One learned rule therefore works whichever way the snake faces32.

Six of the senses come from a run, the three danger flags and the three free runs: the number of free cells walking from the head in one direction before the first obstacle, where an obstacle is a wall or any body cell, tail included. A flood fill is a breadth-first search over empty cells that counts how much room a direction opens up. The senses are the danger flag, the run, the room, the food and tail offsets, and a one-hot heading. A one-hot heading has a 1 for the current heading and 0 elsewhere3334.

\begin{aligned} x_k &= \mathbf{1}[\text{run}_k = 0], \qquad x_{3+k} = \frac{\text{run}_k}{11}, \qquad x_{14+k} = \frac{R_k}{30}, \qquad k \in \{\text{ahead}, \text{left}, \text{right}\} \\ x_{6} &= \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{(f - h) \cdot a}{11}\right), \qquad x_{7} = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{(f - h) \cdot r}{11}\right) \\ x_{8} &= \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{(t - h) \cdot a}{11}\right), \qquad x_{9} = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{(t - h) \cdot r}{11}\right) \\ x_{10+H} &= 1, \quad \text{all other heading inputs } 0 \\ \operatorname{clip}(v) &= \max(-1,\ \min(1,\ v)) \end{aligned}
34

Here \text{run}_k is the run in direction k, R_k the flood-fill count from the cell next to the head in that direction, capped at 30, h the head, f the food, t the tail, a the unit vector of the heading and r the unit vector a quarter turn to its right. The offsets are divided by N - 1 = 11, the largest possible distance across the board, and clipped to [-1, 1]3536. Example. The snake heads east at (6, 6) and the food is at (9, 6). Its forward offset is 3/11 \approx 0.273 and its right offset is 0.

The net is one hidden layer of 16 hidden units, followed by 3 outputs. Each hidden unit takes a weighted sum of the 17 senses plus a bias, a constant offset, and passes it through tanh, a squashing function that maps any number into the range (−1, 1). Each output is a weighted sum of the hidden units plus its own bias. The outputs are in the order left, straight, right, and the largest one is the action. The net does not check for danger itself, so a poor output can send the snake into a wall. Avoiding that has to be learned from the game score37.

h = \tanh(x\,W_1 + b_1), \qquad z = h\,W_2 + b_2, \qquad \text{action} = \operatorname{argmax}_k\, z_k, \qquad 17 \cdot 16 + 16 + 16 \cdot 3 + 3 = 339
3837

Here W_1 is a 17 \times 16 matrix of input weights, b_1 the 16 hidden biases, W_2 a 16 \times 3 matrix of output weights and b_2 the 3 output biases. Ties in z go to the lower index, which is the order left, straight, right. The 339 numbers are stored as one flat vector called a genome, and the genetic algorithm works on that vector39.

    return the index of the largest z                # ties go to the lower index

    z ← h · W2 + b2                                  # 3 outputs: left, straight, right

function SENSE(game):
    occupied ← set of all body cells, tail included
    head ← game.body[0];  heading ← game.heading
    a ← DIRS[heading]                                # ahead
    r ← DIRS[(heading + 1) mod 4]                    # right; left is −r
    for each direction d in (a, −r, r), with index k = 0, 1, 2:
        run ← 0;  cell ← head + d
        while cell is inside the board and cell is not in occupied:
            run ← run + 1
            cell ← cell + d
        x[k] ← 1 if run = 0 else 0                   # danger
        x[3 + k] ← run / 11                          # free run
        x[14 + k] ← ROOM(occupied, head + d) / 30    # room
    x[6], x[7] ← OFFSET(food)
    x[8], x[9] ← OFFSET(tail)
    x[10 + heading] ← 1                              # heading one-hot
    return x

function OFFSET(cell):
    o ← cell − head
    forward ← clip((o · a) / 11)
    right ← clip((o · r) / 11)
    return (forward, right)

function ROOM(occupied, start):
    if start is outside the board or start is in occupied:
        return 0
    seen ← {start};  queue ← [start]
    while queue is not empty and |seen| < 30:      # the cap keeps the search cheap
        cell ← first cell of queue;  remove it from queue
        for each neighbour c of cell:
            if c is outside the board or c is in occupied or c is in seen:
                continue
            add c to seen and to queue
            if |seen| ≥ 30:
                break
    return |seen|

The run counts only cells in a straight line, so it cannot show a pocket that the snake would enter by turning. The room counts every empty cell that a flood fill reaches from the adjacent cell, so it shows the size of the pocket. The cap of 30 keeps each flood fill cheap, since the net only needs to know whether a pocket is small42.

The same arithmetic runs in the browser, in web/js/snake-core.js, so the page's network view matches the benchmark43.

Learning the weights: the genetic algorithm

A genetic algorithm (GA) improves a population of genomes by three operators. Selection picks parents, favouring the fitter genomes. Crossover mixes two parents into a child. Mutation adds small random changes. No gradients are computed. The best genomes are copied unchanged each generation, a practice called elitism, so the best result never gets lost44.

The 60 starting genomes are random: each of their 339 weights is drawn from a normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 0.545. Each generation, 8 boards are drawn from seeds 1000 up to 39,999, and every genome plays the same 8 boards. Its fitness is the mean game score over them. The 4 best genomes are copied unchanged, and the other 56 are children4647.

A parent is chosen by a tournament of size 3. Three genomes are drawn at random with replacement, and the fittest of the three is the parent. Two parents are chosen per child. Uniform crossover then takes each of the 339 weights from one parent or the other with probability ½. Gaussian mutation changes each weight with probability 0.1, by a random amount drawn from a normal distribution with standard deviation 0.1548.

\begin{aligned} \text{parent} &= \operatorname{argmax}_{i \in C} F(g_i), \qquad C \text{: three indices drawn uniformly with replacement} \\ c_j &= m_j\, a_j + (1 - m_j)\, b_j, \qquad m_j \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\tfrac{1}{2}) \\ c_j &\leftarrow c_j + \varepsilon_j, \qquad \varepsilon_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\ 0.15^2) \text{ with probability } 0.1 \end{aligned}
4950

Here a and b are the two parents, c the child, m_j the crossover bit of weight j, and F the fitness. Example. With 60 genomes and 4 elites, each generation makes 56 children, so the population size stays at 6051.

function EVOLVE(generations):
    population ← 60 genomes, each weight drawn from Normal(0, 0.5²)
    repeat generations times:
        fitness ← each genome's mean game score on 8 new boards
        next ← the 4 fittest genomes, unchanged           # elitism
        while |next| < 60:
            a ← TOURNAMENT(fitness, 3);  b ← TOURNAMENT(fitness, 3)
            next ← next + [MUTATE(CROSSOVER(a, b))]
        population ← next
    return the best genome, chosen on the validation boards
function EVOLVE(settings):
    population ← 60 genomes, each of 339 weights drawn from Normal(0, 0.5²)
    best ← none;  candidates ← empty
    for gen ← 1 to 80:                                # the generation count
        seeds ← 8 integers drawn uniformly from 1000 to 39999
        for each genome g in population:
            fitness[g] ← mean over seeds of GAME-SCORE(PLAY(g, seed))
        order ← genomes sorted by fitness, highest first (ties keep the earlier one)
        if fitness[order[1]] > best.fitness:
            best ← (order[1], gen)
        append the record of this generation to the history
        if gen = 80:

            break
        next ← copies of order[1..4]                  # elites, unchanged
        while |next| < 60:
            a ← population[TOURNAMENT(fitness, 3)]
            b ← population[TOURNAMENT(fitness, 3)]
            for each weight j in 1..339:
                m_j ← true with probability 0.5
                child_j ← a_j if m_j else b_j          # uniform crossover
            for each weight j in 1..339:
                if a random draw is below 0.1:

            append child to next

    return the genome with the largest validation score, and its generation

function TOURNAMENT(fitness, k):
    contenders ← k indices drawn uniformly with replacement
    return the contender with the highest fitness

Elitism keeps the top four genomes unchanged, so crossover and mutation cannot destroy them. Because the boards change each generation, a genome's fitness is measured again on new boards, so the fitness of one generation is not comparable with another's. The champion is therefore picked on fixed boards, as in the cross-entropy method. The best genome ever seen and the top five of the last generation are scored on seeds 20000 to 200155354. Those seeds fall inside the training range, so this check is same-distribution, not held-out.

Each genome's fitness is one mean over the 8 boards, so the same boards decide between genomes in a generation. A genome cannot memorise a fixed set of boards, because the next generation draws new ones55.

Baselines: random and greedy

The random policy takes each action with the same probability. It uses its own seeded generator, so a game can be repeated exactly. It senses nothing, so it dies quickly56.

P(\text{left}) = P(\text{straight}) = P(\text{right}) = \tfrac{1}{3}
57

The greedy policy makes a one-step decision with no search. For each safe move it measures the Manhattan distance, the number of grid steps between two cells ignoring walls, from the new head to the food. It takes the move with the smallest distance. It has no learned part.

d(a) = |x_a - x_f| + |y_a - y_f|, \qquad a^\ast = \operatorname{argmin}_{a \in A_{\text{safe}}} d(a)
58

Here (x_a, y_a) is the head's cell after move a and (x_f, y_f) the food. Straight is tried first, so a tie keeps the snake going straight.

58 
function GREEDY(game):
    if game has no food:
        return straight
    best ← straight;  best_distance ← none
    for each action a in (straight, left, right):   # straight first, so ties go straight
        if DIES(game, a):
            continue
        cell ← the head's cell after a
        distance ← |cell.x − food.x| + |cell.y − food.y|
        if best_distance is none or distance < best_distance:
            best ← a;  best_distance ← distance
    return best

Baseline: the breadth-first search planner

The planner is the strongest classic baseline. It uses breadth-first search (BFS) for a shortest route to the food, then for a safety check, and, if needed, for a route to the tail. Its key idea is a safety check. After the snake eats, the tail stays in place, so the head must still be able to reach it. The planner takes the food route only if that holds5960.

The check is a second BFS, from the food to the tail, through the cells the snake would occupy after eating: its body and the food route. If the check fails, the planner follows its tail instead. The tail moves away each step, so a route to it is always open, and this is called tail-chasing. The planner re-plans every step, so it switches to the food route as soon as the check passes. If the planned step is unsafe or no route exists, it picks the safe move that leaves the largest flood-fill region, and if no move is safe it goes straight61.

The page's PLANNER HINT calls the same code on the server, and the route it returns is labelled as food, tail or none62.

    path ← PLAN-ROUTE(game)
    if path is not empty:
        a ← action that turns the head toward the first cell of path   # relative to the heading; none if it is a reversal
        if a is not none and not DIES(game, a):
            return a

function PLAN-ROUTE(game):
    body ← game.body;  head ← the first segment of body;  tail ← the last segment of body
    blocked ← body without its last cell             # the tail will have moved away
    if game.food exists:
        path ← BFS(head, game.food, blocked)
        if path exists:
            new_blocked ← (body ∪ path) without tail
            if BFS(game.food, tail, new_blocked) exists:   # the tail-chasing check
                return path
    return BFS(head, tail, blocked)

function BFS(start, goal, blocked):
    if start = goal:
        return empty path
    parent ← {start: none};  queue ← [start]
    while queue is not empty:                        # cells in order of distance
        cell ← first cell of queue;  remove it from queue
        for each direction d in (north, east, south, west):
            c ← cell + d
            if c is outside the board or c is in parent:
                continue
            if c ≠ goal and c is in blocked:
                continue                              # the goal may be entered even if blocked
            parent[c] ← cell
            if c = goal:
                follow parent links from goal back to start
                return the cells after start, in order
            append c to queue
    return none                                       # no route

function REACHABLE(start, blocked):
    seen ← {start};  queue ← [start]
    while queue is not empty:
        cell ← first cell of queue;  remove it from queue
        for each neighbour c of cell:
            if c is outside the board or c is in seen or c is in blocked:
                continue
            add c to seen and to queue
    return |seen|

The core of the planner is BFS, which finds a shortest route in steps because it reaches each cell first along a shortest path. The planned move is the first step of the route, and the room rule replaces it only when that step is unsafe or no route exists64.

How these were chosen: by trial, not by a systematic sweep. The first version used three fixed training boards and learned to memorise them: its best generation scored 6.0 apples per game on those boards, against 2.05 on the benchmark boards. Fresh boards each generation fixed most of that. The starvation cap was first set to four laps, then cut to two to keep looping games cheap; the benchmark uses the same cap for every agent. The population and generation counts were set by the time budget. Two longer runs (v3a, v3b: 200 generations, population 100) did not beat v2. v3a's penalty of 2.0 collapsed into early death. v3b, with the penalty back at 0.5, reached 13.22 apples, within noise of v2's 13.69 and not better, so v2 stays the live champion.

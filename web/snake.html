HOW THE EVALUATOR CHOOSES
The snake tries each safe move, and for the position it would reach it reads eight features: whether the move eats, how near the food is by walking distance, whether the food is reachable, how much of the board it can reach, whether that room can hold its body, whether it can still reach its tail, how many exits its head has, and how long it is. The move with the largest weighted sum wins. The rules, the features and the choice are hand-built. The eight weights are the only thing that was learned.

THE PLAYERS

HOW IT WORKS

Each turn the snake picks left, straight, or right with a small neural network of 339 weights. Nobody wrote those weights. A genetic algorithm bred them: play a generation of snakes on fresh boards, keep the best, mix and mutate them, and repeat. The evolved snake learned to eat, but it is still well behind a greedy rule and a BFS planner on the same held-out boards.

Evolving the weights

New to this? Think of breeding dogs for herding. You keep the best of each litter, cross them, add small random changes, and repeat. Nobody explains herding to the dogs; the good ones just get to breed more.

Each generation, 60 genomes (lists of 339 numbers) play 8 games on the same fresh boards. A game scores apples eaten, plus a small bonus for surviving, minus a penalty for starving. Then: the 4 best are copied unchanged, and the other 56 are children. A parent is the best of 3 random genomes (tournament). Each weight comes from one parent or the other with equal chance (uniform crossover), and about one weight in ten is nudged by a small random amount.

The network and its 17 senses

New to this? A network is a chain of weighted sums. Each input is multiplied by a weight, the results are added up, and a squashing function keeps them between −1 and 1. The output with the largest value wins, so the snake turns that way.

The snake senses the board in its own frame, so "ahead" means where it is facing: danger ahead, left, and right (is the next cell blocked?); free run in each direction; the food's offset ahead and to the right; the tail's offset; its heading; and the room it can reach in each direction, counted by a flood fill up to 30 cells. The 17 inputs feed 16 hidden units (tanh), which feed 3 outputs.

Baseline: greedy

New to this? Greedy is the simplest sensible rule: look at the three possible moves, throw out the ones that crash, and take the one that ends closest to the food.

It needs no learning and no lookahead. It is the bar an evolved net should clear, and it wins on apples per game against the evolved net here.

Baseline: BFS planner with tail-chasing

New to this? A breadth-first search (BFS) tries every route one step deeper at a time, so the first route it finds to the food is a shortest one. The snake must also not trap itself after eating.

The planner finds a shortest path to the food over empty cells. Before taking it, it checks that after eating, the head can still reach the tail. If not, it follows its own tail (which always moves away) until the food route is safe. It is the strongest classic baseline here, and it still starves on some boards.

How these were chosen: by trial, not by a systematic sweep. The first version used three fixed training boards and learned to memorise them (6.0 apples on those boards, 2.05 on new ones). Fresh boards each generation fixed most of that. The starvation cap was first set to four laps, then cut to two to keep looping games cheap; the benchmark uses the same cap for every agent. The population and generation counts were set by the time budget. Two longer runs (v3a, v3b: 200 generations, population 100) did not beat v2. v3a's penalty of 2.0 collapsed into early death. v3b, with the penalty back at 0.5, reached 13.22 apples, within noise of v2's 13.69 and not better, so v2 stays the live champion. No parameter was tuned on the benchmark seeds.

Which way the net turns: over the first 40 benchmark boards (seeds 50000 to 50039) the net made 19,829 moves: 17,033 straight (85.9%), 2,793 left (14.1%), and 3 right (0.02%). The mean score above hides this, so the turning pattern is nearly one-sided. The counts are committed in results/snake_net_actions.json (python -m snake actions --write).

Results on held-out boards: 200 seeded games per agent on 12 × 12, seeds 50000 to 50199, the same boards for every agent. Random scores 0.16 apples, greedy 20.12, the BFS planner 56.13, and the evolved net (v2) 13.69. So the evolved snake clearly beats random and learned to eat, but it does not beat greedy, and it dies in 38.5% of games. Greedy dies in every game, but it eats more before it does. Treat the evolved row as a result, not a win.

HOW IT WORKS

The rover plans on what it has seen so far, so the best route can change every time its sensor finds a wall. This page runs two planners on the same discoveries. D* Lite keeps its search results and repairs only what a new wall affects. A* forgets everything and searches from the rover's cell again. Both always find a shortest route for what the rover knows, so the costs match; what differs is the work.

1. The rover plans on a belief

New to this? The rover does not know where the walls are. It starts with a belief: every cell is free until its sensor covers it. The sensor sees a square of cells around the rover (radius 1 to 4), so the neighbours of the rover are always known before it moves, and it never walks into a wall. Each new wall is a change to the belief, and each change can make the current route wrong.

Walls are edges with infinite cost. A cell that becomes a wall makes its four edges impossible, so the planner must route around it. Costs are counted in steps: entering a free cell costs 1.

2. D* Lite keeps two numbers per cell

New to this? D* Lite searches backwards, from the goal. Each cell stores g, the cost it currently believes it has to the goal, and rhs, a one-step lookahead: the best neighbour's g plus the step cost. When g equals rhs the cell is consistent. The rover then moves to the neighbour with the lowest c + g, which walks a shortest path.

A new wall makes some rhs values wrong. Only those cells are pushed onto a priority queue, keyed by [min(g, rhs) + h(rover, cell) + km, min(g, rhs)]. The search pops cells until the rover's cell is consistent and nothing cheaper is waiting. Cells the change does not reach are never touched.

3. km: keys stay valid as the rover moves

New to this? The queue keys measure distance from the rover. When the rover moves, every key would go stale, and rebuilding the queue would throw away the reuse. D* Lite avoids that with one offset, km. After each repair it grows by the distance the rover moved, so the keys already in the queue remain correct without being rewritten.

The queue is a binary heap with lazy deletion: a cell that is removed keeps its old entry, and the entry is skipped when it reaches the top. That is cheaper than deleting from the middle of a heap.

4. A* from scratch: the baseline

New to this? A* is the standard shortest-path search: expand the cell with the lowest f = g + h, where h is a guess of the remaining distance. Here h is the Manhattan distance, which never overestimates on a grid, so the first time a cell is expanded its cost is final.

The baseline runs that search again from the rover's cell every time the sensor changes the map. It is simple, and it is always correct, but it pays for the whole search each time. The chart shows how much that adds up to.

5. Reading the heat map and the counts

New to this? Choose g or rhs in HEAT MAP to see D* Lite's numbers across the maze: cyan is close to the goal, pink is far. Cells outlined in green were expanded by the last replan of the route you selected. An expansion is one cell taken off the queue and processed. The counters add up every expansion so far, including the first plan on the empty map.

COST CHECK compares the two planners at every replan: they must report the same cost from the rover's cell. It has to read 0 mismatches.

The sizes and densities are the ones the benchmark sweeps, so the table on this page is the same table the CLI prints. The default radius of 2 reveals a 5x5 square, so the rover learns walls a couple of cells ahead of itself; the slider changes that. In a headless Chromium run, one complete route on a 61x61 map at 30% walls took about 160 ms with both planners running. That covers the planning only, not drawing. The 20% map at 41x41 took under 10 ms. Those are single measurements on one core, so treat them as orders of magnitude.

HOW IT WORKS

Every optimizer here does the same thing: look at the slope where it stands, then move downhill. They differ in how far they move and which past slopes they remember. The race starts all of them from one point with the same gradient noise, so the differences come from the update rules.

Gradient descent (SGD)

New to this? Stand on a hillside in fog. Feel which way is steepest uphill, and take a step the other way. Take a small step and you crawl; take a big one and you may overshoot the valley floor and land on the far side.

The gradient ∇f(x) points uphill, so one step moves against it:

  • x ← x − η ∇f(x)
  • η is the learning rate (the step size).

On a bowl this converges. On a ravine it zigzags: a step large enough to make progress along the gentle direction overshoots across the steep one, so the iterate flips sign there while it crawls along the valley.

Momentum and Nesterov

New to this? A ball rolling down a bumpy slope keeps some speed. It does not reverse every time a small bump pushes it sideways, so it builds up speed along the valley and damps the zigzag across it.

Momentum keeps a velocity v that remembers past steps:

  • v ← β v − η ∇f(x), then x ← x + v
  • Nesterov takes the gradient at the look-ahead point x + β v instead of at x, so it sees the slope where it is about to be.

With β near 1 the effective step is η / (1 − β): with the defaults, 0.02 / 0.1 = 0.2 per unit of slope along a consistent direction.

RMSProp

New to this? Some directions have steep slopes and some have gentle ones. RMSProp divides each direction's step by how big its recent slopes have been, so steep directions take smaller steps and gentle ones larger.

It keeps a running average of squared gradients, s, per coordinate:

  • s ← ρ s + (1 − ρ) g²
  • x ← x − η g / (√s + ε)

With a fixed η the step never shrinks to zero: near a minimum it hovers at a size set by η, which is why RMSProp can stall on the ravine without a decaying step.

Adam

New to this? Adam combines the two ideas above. It keeps a velocity (the average slope) and a scale (the average squared slope), and corrects both for starting at zero, so its first steps are not tiny.

With g the gradient, t the step number:

  • m ← β₁ m + (1 − β₁) g, v ← β₂ v + (1 − β₂) g²
  • m̂ = m / (1 − β₁ᵗ), v̂ = v / (1 − β₂ᵗ) (bias correction)
  • x ← x − η m̂ / (√v̂ + ε)

Each step is about η in size per coordinate whatever the gradient's scale, so Adam is less sensitive to the scale of the loss than SGD. AdaGrad (in the race, optional) is the same idea without the decay: its scale only grows, so its steps shrink over time.

Conditioning and saddle points

New to this? A landscape is well conditioned when all directions are about equally steep, so one step size fits all of them. It is ill conditioned when some directions are much steeper than others, like a narrow canyon.

The condition number is the ratio of the largest to smallest curvature (the Hessian's eigenvalues). The ravine has curvature 25 across and 1 along, so κ = 25. Gradient descent's safe step is below 2 / 25, while the step that moves quickly along the valley is much larger.

A saddle has zero gradient but is not a minimum: the curvature is positive along one direction and negative along another. The lab's "settled" test checks the Hessian for this, so the origin of the saddle surface is never reported as a minimum.

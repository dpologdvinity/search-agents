HOW IT WORKS

Each creature is a soft body: point masses joined by springs, some of them muscles whose rest length pulses. A genetic algorithm scores every creature by how far its centre of mass travels to the right in 10 simulated seconds, then breeds the next generation from the best of each species. Nothing about walking is coded in: the gaits you see come from selection on distance alone.

Genetic algorithm (the loop)

New to this? Breed the way farmers breed animals: keep the best, mix two of them, and make small random changes. Repeat for many generations. Nobody writes the answer down; the population finds it by trying things and keeping what works.

One generation: simulate all 80 creatures for 10 s at 240 steps per second; fitness is the centre-of-mass travel in x, less a spin penalty, plus a bonus for speed in the second half; speciate; breed. The next population is made of the elites (the best of each species of 5 or more, unchanged), then children of tournament picks from the top half of each species, with each species' share of the 80 slots set by its adjusted fitness.

Genome and encoding

New to this? A genome is the creature's recipe: where each point goes, what joins which, and how each spring behaves. The recipe is short enough to paste into a chat message, so a creature can be shared as one line of text.

A genome is a list of nodes (x, y in metres, relative to the body) and springs (two node indices, rest length, stiffness k, damping c, a muscle flag, and for muscles an amplitude, frequency and phase). The text form is w1|x,y;…|a,b,rest,k,c,muscle,amp,freq,phase;…. Every gene sits on a 0.0001 grid, so the text reads back to the same genome, bit for bit, in JavaScript and Python.

Bodies have 2 to 12 nodes and at most 40 springs. Every body is connected, has at least one muscle, and has no duplicate springs. A repair step enforces this after every operator: it links any unreached node to the nearest reached one.

Selection and crossover

New to this? Crossover is like taking the left half of one parent's recipe and the right half of another's. Each child gets some of each parent's traits, so good traits from two creatures can combine in one.

Parents are chosen by tournament: three random members of the top half, the fittest wins. Crossover, with probability 0.6, is aligned by node index, which is the only stable way to line up two bodies: node i of one parent is node i of the other. The fitter parent sets the node count. A node both parents have is taken from one of them at random. A spring both parents have (the same two endpoints) takes its genes from one parent at random. Springs that only the fitter parent has are kept; springs that only the weaker parent has are dropped, the rule NEAT uses for disjoint genes.

Mutation

New to this? Mutation is a copying mistake that is sometimes useful. Most children get small nudges (a node moves a little, a muscle beats a little faster). Rarely a whole new point or link appears, and occasionally a spring turns into a muscle, or the other way.

Each node and each spring jitters with probability rate/2 (rate is the mutation slider, 0.5 by default): a node by up to ±0.06 m, rest length ±10%, stiffness and damping ±20%, amplitude ±0.05, frequency ±20%, phase ±0.5 rad. Structural operators fire with their own chance: add a node (0.15 × rate), remove a node (0.10), add a spring (0.20), remove a spring (0.15), toggle a spring between muscle and plain (0.20). A new node joins a random existing node and its nearest neighbour.

Speciation (keeping novel bodies alive)

New to this? A new body plan usually scores worse than the old champions at first, so it would die out before it has time to improve. Grouping similar creatures into species, and sharing each group's score among its members, gives an odd shape its own quota of breeding slots.

Two creatures are compared by a distance: 0.4 per difference in node count, 0.3 per difference in spring count, the difference in muscle fraction, and one minus the Jaccard similarity of their node-index pairs. A creature joins the first species within 1.2 of its representative, or founds a new one. Each species' members share the fitness (divided by the species size), and the population is split between species in proportion to the summed shared fitness.

Springs and muscles (the physics)

New to this? A spring pulls its two ends together when stretched and pushes them apart when squeezed. A muscle is a spring whose natural length keeps changing, so it pulls and pushes in rhythm, and that rhythm is what makes a body move.

Each spring applies Hooke's law along the line between its nodes: F = k(L − L0) + c·(dL/dt), where L is the current length and L0 the rest length. The damping term c·(dL/dt) removes energy as the spring stretches and relaxes. For a muscle, L0(t) = rest · (1 + amp · sin(2πft + φ)), so the muscle lengthens and shortens with its own frequency and phase. Its colour in the arena is the tension: pink when it pulls the ends together past its current rest, cyan when it pushes them apart.

Each node has mass 1 kg, gravity acts on every node, and a small linear drag (0.1 per second) slows the nodes and damps the springs. Drag does not prevent the contact limit described under integration.

Ground contact and friction

New to this? Friction is what lets a foot push against the floor without sliding away. Sticky feet grip, slippery feet slide. The simulation decides how much each foot can grip from how hard it is pressed down.

A node below the ground is put back on it. Its normal speed is reversed with the restitution (0.2) if the impact is faster than 0.5 m/s, and otherwise stopped, so resting feet do not jitter. The tangential speed is cut with a Coulomb limit of (1 + restitution) × normal speed: a foot sticks if its sliding speed is within the static coefficient (μs = 1.2 × friction) times that limit, otherwise it loses the kinetic coefficient (friction, 0.8 by default) times that limit.

Integration and its limits

New to this? A simulation moves time forward in small steps, and each step uses the last positions to work out the next. If the steps are too big, the springs overshoot and the body blows up. The steps here are small, so each overshoot is small, but that is not the whole story: see the known limit below.

Semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler at a fixed step of 1/240 s: forces from the positions at the start of the step, then velocities, then positions, then ground contact. Velocities are clamped to 30 m/s. A NaN makes the creature's fitness 0 and marks it exploded. A body whose centre of mass drops below −1.5 m has fallen into a gap, and its score is frozen where it fell.

Known limit: nodes pass through each other. There is no contact between nodes, so two nodes of one body can cross. A spring's force is k(L − L0) along the line between its ends. When the ends cross, the length L passes through zero and the direction of that line flips, so the force pushes the wrong way. Undamped, this adds energy. In a check with one spring (k 200, rest 0.4 m, released at 1.0 m apart, no gravity, no ground, no drag), the energy rose from 35.8 J to 59.6 J, a gain of 67%. A spring that never comes near zero length keeps its energy within about 5%. Drag damps the effect but does not prevent it. The physics has not been changed, because the evolved champions depend on it; a minimum-separation contact would fix it and would need a new evolution run.

The sine used by the muscles is a polynomial in basic arithmetic rather than Math.sin, so JavaScript and Python agree bit for bit, which matters because a chaotic body amplifies any last-bit difference.

How these were chosen: The physics constants (step, drag, velocity clamp, restitution threshold, fall line) are standard for explicit simulation and were set by hand; none was tuned against the node-crossing limit. The genetic parameters (crossover 0.6, tournament 3, half the top as parents, elites of 5, threshold 1.2, the compatibility weights) follow the usual NEAT-style defaults and were set by hand. The mutation sizes and the speed bonus are judgement calls. The spin penalty began at 0.25 m per radian; a two-node body then tumbled sixteen turns for 99 m and scored 79, so it went to 1.00. The checks were the 300-generation runs below, and a friction sweep on the inchworm (friction 0.1 to 1.2). No sweep over these values is committed. Gravity and friction are the UI's to change, and each change alters what evolves.

Terrains

Flat: a plain floor. Hills: a ramp-in from 3 m, then two sine waves (0.35 m and 0.12 m amplitude). Steps: 0.15 m risers every 1.2 m from 3 m, up to eight. Gaps: 0.9 m holes every 3.2 m from 4 m, where a body that falls through is scored where it fell. The start is always 2 m from the origin, on flat ground.

The two best bodies are different body plans from different seeds: a 10-node skipper that gets off the ground once, and an 11-node low crawler that never leaves the ground by more than 0.31 m.

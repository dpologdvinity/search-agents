HOW IT WORKS

For each falling piece the agent tries every rotation and every column, scores the board that would result as a weighted sum of nine features, and plays the best one. Only the nine weights were learned. The search, the features and the game rules are hand-written.

Try every placement

New to this? Before a piece lands, try it in every spot and keep the one that looks best afterwards. Trying everything is slow for chess, but for Tetris a piece has only a few dozen resting places.

A piece has up to four rotations, and each can sit in up to ten columns: about 34 resting spots on an empty board. Each one drops straight down and locks. The board is stored as one bitmask per row, so a full row is detected and cleared with a single comparison. With lookahead on, the six best placements are each followed by the best placement of the preview piece, and the two scores are added.

Score the result

New to this? The score is a checklist with weights. Each item is a count, such as holes or bumps, multiplied by how much that item should matter. Summing the products gives one number per placement.

Nine features are read off the board after the lock and the line clear: landing height, eroded piece cells, row and column transitions, holes, cumulative wells, aggregate height, bumpiness, and completed lines. The score is their weighted sum. The agent takes the highest. The first six come from the classic Dellacherie set; the other three (aggregate height, bumpiness, lines cleared) are simple board-shape counts.

Genetic algorithm (v2)

New to this? Start with many random guesses. Keep the better ones, mix pairs of them, nudge the results a little, and repeat for many generations. Weights that play better survive more often.

Population 16, 16 generations. Each genome plays 6 games on the 10-row board, capped at 2,500 pieces. Each generation uses fresh seeds that no other generation sees. Selection is by tournament, crossover is arithmetic, mutation is Gaussian, and the two best genomes are copied unchanged. This set was worse than the hand-picked weights on held-out games.

Cross-entropy method (v3)

New to this? Picture a cloud of possible weight sets. Try many of them, keep the best tenth, move the cloud's centre to them, and keep it wide enough to keep exploring. Repeat until the cloud stops moving.

This is the noisy cross-entropy method of Szita and Lorincz (2006), the approach usually used for Tetris. A Gaussian over the nine weights starts centred on the hand-picked set. Each iteration samples 20 weight sets, plays 3 fresh games each to game over (cap 100,000 pieces, matching the benchmark), keeps the top 15%, refits the mean and variance to them, and adds a shrinking amount of extra variance. The run was stopped after 6 iterations for time.

Weights only matter relative to each other, since the agent takes the highest score, so read them together: a large weight on one feature can be offset by a large weight on another.

Training

Training uses a 10-row board, a different board from the 20-row game you can play here. Training games use seeds from 200000 upward for the cross-entropy run and from 1000 upward for the GA, both separate from the benchmark seeds. The cross-entropy run had 20 samples per iteration, 3 games each, 6 iterations completed, and the final mean is the distribution's centre after iteration 6 (training fitness is the last elite mean, 6,869 lines). The GA had 16 genomes, 6 games each, 2,500-piece cap, and 16 generations (training fitness on its best genome's own seeds, 804.5 lines, which is optimistic). Both runs are logged in results/.
